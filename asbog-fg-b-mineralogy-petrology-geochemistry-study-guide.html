1.2

Classification, simplified

Full Dana/Strunz classification has ~9 chemical classes. Collapse to a working hierarchy:

  1. Silicates — ~90% of crustal minerals; nearly all your points live here M 81
  2. Carbonates — calcite, dolomite (sedimentary/diagenetic)
  3. Oxides/hydroxides — magnetite, hematite (ore + weathering)
  4. Sulfides — pyrite, galena, sphalerite, chalcopyrite (ore)
  5. Sulfates, halides — evaporite minerals
  6. Native elements, phosphates — low volume, high economic/biogenic relevance

The single highest-value table in this category — silicate structure predicts cleavage, hardness, and weathering behavior. Marshak's reference figure: M 83, Fig. 3.9. 1.4  (B-1.2 cont., B-1.3, B-1.4)

Hazardous minerals, optical properties, and lab identification methods

Added after cross-checking the guide against ASBOG's own detailed Domain B knowledge-base document (not just the Candidate Handbook summary) — this entire subsection was previously missing.

Optical properties beyond streak/luster — B-1.2 also names luminescence and crystal-structure anisotropy specifically. K 26, "Properties Based on Interaction with Light"

  • Fluorescence — mineral glows under UV light, stops when the UV source is removed (fluorite is the namesake example — it's in your Tier 3 list already).
  • Phosphorescence — like fluorescence, but the glow persists briefly after the UV source is removed.
  • Anisotropy — optical properties (e.g., refractive index) vary with crystal direction, a direct consequence of non-isometric crystal structure. Isometric minerals are optically isotropic (properties are the same in all directions); every other crystal system is anisotropic to some degree. This is the physical basis for how polarized-light microscopy identifies minerals in thin section.

Hazardous minerals — genuinely absent from the guide until now, and worth taking seriously given ASBOG's stated mission is public health and safety, not just technical competence.

  • Asbestos isn't one mineral — it's a regulatory/commercial term covering six fibrous ("asbestiform") mineral varieties: one serpentine-group mineral (chrysotile — by far the most heavily mined) and five amphibole-group varieties (amosite, crocidolite, tremolite, actinolite, anthophyllite, all asbestiform habits of minerals that also occur in ordinary, non-hazardous forms). K 527, Box 19.2, "Asbestos: A Mixture and Mix-up of Minerals"
  • Health effects: asbestosis (lung scarring), lung cancer, and mesothelioma (cancer of the lung/abdominal lining) from inhaled fibers. K 531
  • Silicosis — lung disease from inhaling fine crystalline silica (quartz) dust, an occupational hazard in mining, quarrying, and stone-cutting. Gap — checked five sources (Marshak, Klein & Dutrow, White, Earle, USBR field manual); genuinely absent from all, general occupational-health knowledge
  • Pb-bearing minerals — galena (PbS, already Tier 3) is the primary natural source of environmental lead contamination near mine sites/tailings; lead toxicity (neurological, especially in children) is the standard concern. Gap — general toxicology knowledge, not textbook-specific

Material characterization methods — Klein & Dutrow devotes a full chapter to this, which directly matches B-1.4's example list almost item-for-item: K 307, Ch. 14, "Analytical and Imaging Methods in Mineral Science"

MethodWhat it tells you
XRD (X-ray Diffraction)Identifies mineral structure/species from its characteristic diffraction pattern — the standard method for confirming "which mineral is this" when visual ID isn't enough.

02 / Petrology

Igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks

2.1

Igneous rocks

Classification is 2-dimensional: composition (x-axis) × texture (y-axis). Marshak's own classification chart: M 113, Fig. 4.13; texture types M 111, Fig. 4.12; overview spread M 112, Geology at a Glance

Felsic (>65% SiO₂)IntermediateMaficUltramafic
Intrusive (coarse)GraniteDioriteGabbroPeridotite
Extrusive (fine)RhyoliteAndesiteBasaltKomatiite (rare)
Original diagram — not a reproduction of Marshak's figure. Boxed feature in the text: M 105, Box 4.1. Minerals crystallizing first (high T) are also least stable at the surface — same order as the Goldich weathering series, just read in reverse.

Igneous process vocabulary: fractional crystallization, partial melting, assimilation, magma mixing, crystal settling — definitions, not derivations, at FG level. M 99–104, §4.2–4.3

Phase diagrams, exsolution, nucleation (B-2.2) — Marshak doesn't cover these by name; Klein & Dutrow does, in a dedicated chapter: K 249, Ch. 11, "Mineral Stability and Phase Diagrams"

  • Phase diagram — a plot (often T vs. composition, or T vs. P) showing which mineral phase(s) are stable under given conditions; Bowen's diagram above is itself a simplified phase-relationship diagram.
  • Exsolution — a single homogeneous mineral, stable at high temperature, "unmixes" into two distinct mineral phases as it cools and solubility drops (classic example: perthite, exsolved intergrowths of K-feldspar and Na-plagioclase). K 276, "Exsolution"
  • Nucleation — the initial formation of a tiny stable crystal from melt/solution, the first step before crystal growth. Gap — not treated distinctly as a named concept in either source at the igneous-process level; standard petrology/crystallization theory
2.2  (B-2.3, B-2.4)

Igneous petrogenesis, tectonic settings & volcanism

Added after cross-checking against ASBOG's detailed Domain B document — previously the guide covered what igneous rocks are but not where/why they form, or volcano types/eruption behavior at all.

Tectonic settings of igneous activity — Marshak covers this directly, tied to the volcanism chapter: M 135, "Relation of Volcanism to Plate Tectonics"

SettingTypical magmaExample
Mid-ocean ridge spreading centersMafic (basalt/gabbro) — decompression melting of mantle peridotiteMid-Atlantic Ridge
Subduction zones (continental arc)Intermediate (andesite/diorite) — flux melting from subducted slab's water contentAndes, Cascades
Subduction zones (island arc)Mafic to intermediateAleutians, Japan
HotspotsMafic (basalt) — melting over a stationary mantle plume, independent of plate boundariesHawaii, Yellowstone
Continental rift zonesBimodal (basalt + rhyolite)East African Rift

Volcano types and eruption style — directly covered, Ch. 5: M 126, "Shield, Cinder Cone, and Composite Volcanoes"

  • Shield volcano — broad, gently-sloping, built from many low-viscosity basaltic lava flows (effusive eruptions). Example: Mauna Loa.
  • Cinder cone — small, steep, built from a single eruption's basaltic cinders/lapilli (fountaining eruptions).
  • Stratovolcano (composite volcano) — large, steep-sided, alternating lava flows and pyroclastic layers from more viscous, gas-rich intermediate-to-felsic magma. Example: Mt. St. Helens. Associated with the most explosive, hazardous eruptions. M 137, "Beware: Volcanoes Are Hazards!"

Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) — a logarithmic 0–8 scale ranking eruption magnitude, based on ejecta volume, plume height, and duration. Devised by Newhall & Self (USGS, 1982) — not in any of the six geology textbooks/manuals checked, but it's a standard, citable, widely-used index outside those texts. Newhall, C.G. & Self, S. (1982), J. Geophys. Res. 87(C2)

Related, and actually covered: Plinian eruption — the term for the most violently explosive eruption style (tall ash/gas column reaching the stratosphere), the qualitative top end of what VEI measures numerically. E Ch.4, "Volcanism"

Chemical/biochemical: Limestone (calcite), Dolostone (diagenetic dolomitization of limestone), Chert (biogenic or chemical SiO₂), Evaporites (gypsum, anhydrite, halite), Coal. M 164–172, §6.2

Mineralogy tied to environmental setting (B-3.2) — the same mineral in a sedimentary rock is a clue to the depositional environment: heavy, chemically resistant minerals (zircon, magnetite, garnet) concentrate in high-energy beach/placer settings; clay minerals dominate quiet low-energy settings (lakes, deep marine); evaporite minerals require arid, restricted-basin settings. Gap — this framing isn't spelled out as a unified concept in Marshak, though each piece appears separately

Diagenesis gets brief treatment in this textbook relative to how ASBOG weights it M 185 — this is a strength area for you already, so the gap matters less, but don't assume Marshak's coverage alone is sufficient depth here. Redoximorphic features (B-3.3) — mottling, color banding, and Fe/Mn concentration or depletion zones that record alternating oxidizing/reducing conditions during diagenesis (or soil formation) — aren't named in Marshak either. Gap — general soil-science/diagenesis knowledge, directly in your wheelhouse

Field weathering-grade terminology — a practical, real-world framework worth knowing exists even if FG is unlikely to test it directly: the Bureau of Reclamation's field manual defines a 9-step weathering scale (W1 "fresh" through W9 "decomposed, resembles soil") based on discoloration, fracture condition, and hammer-ring test, plus a parallel hardness scale (H1–H7) — this is PG/field-application territory, not FG recall, but grounds the abstract "weathering" concept in something concrete. USBR Field Manual, Vol. 1, Ch. 4, Table 4-4

Sedimentary structures: graded bedding, cross-bedding M 175, Fig. 6.13, ripple marks, mud cracks M 177, Fig. 6.15, flaser/wavy/lenticular bedding, bioturbation.

2.4

Metamorphic rocks

Three controls: contact (localized heat, non-foliated), regional (heat + directed pressure, foliated), dynamic/cataclastic (fault-zone deformation). Causes/processes: M 190–193, §7.2

Original diagram. Marshak's equivalent figure — literally titled "Intensity of metamorphism is indicated by metamorphic grade": M 199, Fig. 7.8. Slate example: M 194, Fig. 7.4

Non-foliated: Limestone→Marble, Quartz sandstone→Quartzite, Basalt (contact)→Hornfels.

Grade/index minerals (Barrovian sequence): Chlorite → Biotite → Garnet → Staurolite → Kyanite → Sillimanite. Combined with the Al₂SiO₅ polymorph triangle (Mineralogy §1.3), this answers both "what grade" and "what P-T regime" from mineral assemblage alone.

Metamorphic facies M 200, Box 7.1 — boxed feature, easy to skip if flipping fast. Normal geothermal gradient: Zeolite → Greenschist → Amphibolite → Granulite. Subduction-related (high P, low T): Blueschist → Eclogite. Environments overview: M 198–207, §7.4 & p.204–205

03 / Geochemistry

Earth composition, weathering, isotopes, and alteration

3.1  (B-5.1, B-5.2, B-5.4)

Earth's chemical composition, element classification & stability diagrams

Added after cross-checking against ASBOG's detailed Domain B document — this was a fully missing subsection, not a refinement of existing content.

Composition of Earth's layers — whole-Earth vs. crust-only composition are genuinely different, and mixing them up is an easy mistake: M 27, Fig. 1.12 (whole Earth) vs. M 31, Fig. 1.16 (crust only)

ReservoirDominant elements (by mass)Note
Whole EarthFe 32%, O 30%, Si 15%, Mg 14%Iron dominates because the dense core is iron-rich
Crust onlyO most abundant, then Si, Al, FeOxygen dominates — crust is depleted in the iron that sank to the core
Core~85% Fe, ~5% Ni, ~10% lighter element (O/Si/S)Composition inferred from meteorites + seismic behavior, not direct sampling

Core composition detail: M 258, Interlude D

Element classification and associations (B-5.2) — standard periodic-table groupings, worth knowing by geochemical behavior rather than just position. Halides and noble/inert gases are directly covered in an open-access source: E Ch.2, §2.1 & §2.3

  • Halides — Group 17 (F, Cl, Br, I) plus At; anion in halide minerals (halite, fluorite). E §2.3, "Mineral Groups"
  • Noble (inert) gases — Group 18 (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe); full outer electron shell means no tendency to bond — explicitly why K-Ar dating works (radiogenic Ar stays trapped rather than reacting away). E §2.1, "Electrons, Protons, Neutrons, and Atoms"
  • Transition elements — the d-block metals (Fe, Cu, Zn, Ni, etc.); variable oxidation states make them geochemically mobile and central to ore deposits/redox chemistry. Now solidly covered — Luther devotes two full chapters to this group specifically. L 237, Ch.8 "Introduction to Transition Metals" · L 356, Ch.10 "Transition Metals in Natural Systems"

Stability diagrams (B-5.4) — squarely in your own background. Klein & Dutrow covers this with a full chapter: K 249, Ch. 11, "Mineral Stability and Phase Diagrams", Eh-pH diagrams specifically K 262

  • Eh-pH (Pourbaix) diagrams — plot redox potential (Eh) against pH to show which mineral/ionic species is stable under given water-chemistry conditions. Standard tool for predicting mineral stability in weathering, acid mine drainage, and groundwater geochemistry.
  • P-T diagrams — pressure-temperature stability fields; this is the same logic behind the Al₂SiO₅ polymorph triangle (Mineralogy §1.3) and metamorphic facies (Petrology §2.4) — a P-T diagram is just that logic made explicit and graphical.
  • T-X diagrams — temperature vs. composition; this is what Bowen's Reaction Series actually is, formalized (Petrology §2.1).

Note the pattern: three of ASBOG's named diagram types (T-X, P-T, Eh-pH) are formalized versions of concepts already in this guide under other names — Bowen's series, the metamorphic facies/polymorph logic, and redox weathering, respectively.

3.2

Fundamentals of geochemical reactions and weathering

This overlaps your existing expertise — treat this as reframing into ASBOG vocabulary rather than new learning. Weathering overview: M 148–162, Interlude B, esp. §B.2 M 150 and the "Geology at a Glance" spread M 154–155

  • Goldich weathering stability series — inverse of Bowen's crystallization order (Petrology §2.1). Olivine/Ca-plagioclase weather fastest; quartz/muscovite most resistant. Named and sourced (S.S. Goldich, 1938) — covered directly in an open-access source. LT — Mineralogy (Perkins et al.), Geosciences LibreTexts, §7.1.3 "Ease of Weathering"
  • Redox potential / master variables — pH, Eh, and ionic strength as the "master variables" controlling aqueous geochemical behavior (directly underlies the Eh-pH diagrams in §3.1). L 11, Ch.1
  • Congruent vs. incongruent dissolution — congruent = full dissolution (halite, calcite in acid); incongruent = partial dissolution + new solid residue (feldspar → clay + ions). Gap — checked six sources (Marshak, Klein & Dutrow, White, Earle, USBR field manual, Luther); terminology not named in any, though the underlying feldspar-to-clay process is covered (Earle Ch.5, Luther's dissolution chemistry) without this specific vocabulary
  • Redox / pyrite oxidation — appears directly in ASBOG's own sample questions (pyrite + water at surface → acidic runoff). ASBOG Handbook, Appendix 3, Q1
  • Solubility controls — pH/Eh govern mineral solubility; carbonate solubility rises with decreasing pH and increasing CO₂ (karst chemistry — your territory).
  • Clay mineral sequence — smectite → illite → kaolinite as a weathering-intensity/leaching gradient (kaolinite = most weathered end-member).
