01 / Structural Geology

Deformation, brittle & ductile structures, fabrics

1.1

Stress, strain & mechanical behavior

Deformation involves up to three components: displacement (change in location), rotation (change in orientation), and distortion, which geologists call strain (change in shape) Marshak (2013) 267–268, Fig. 9.3.

Stress is force per unit area — the same force concentrated on a small area produces a larger stress than the same force spread over a large area (the "standing on one can vs. standing on a board over 100 cans" example) Marshak (2013) 269, Fig. 9.6a. Three kinds of stress act on rock bodies:

StressDefinitionResulting strain
CompressionSqueezing — opposing forces pushed togetherShortening
TensionPulling apart — opposite ends pulled in opposite directionsStretching
ShearOne part of a rock body slides sideways past anotherShear strain (angular distortion)

Pressure is a special case — equal push from all sides — that changes volume but not shape Marshak (2013) 270, Fig. 9.6b–e.

Original diagram, following Marshak's stress-type breakdown Marshak (2013) 269–270, Fig. 9.6

Brittle vs. ductile deformation — brittle: bonds break permanently (cracking, like a dropped plate). Ductile: bonds break and re-form as grains flow (like squeezed dough), no visible fracture Marshak (2013) 267. Four controls on which mode a rock takes:

  • Temperature — warmer rock deforms more ductilely; heat softens material
  • Pressure/confining pressure — higher pressure (greater depth) favors ductile behavior; it suppresses fragmentation
  • Strain rate — sudden stress → brittle; slow, sustained stress → ductile (hit a marble bench with a hammer vs. leaving it alone for a century)
  • Composition — some minerals/rocks are inherently softer (halite deforms ductilely under conditions where granite deforms brittlely)

In typical continental crust, rock behaves brittlely above ~10–15 km depth and ductilely below — the brittle-ductile transition. Earthquakes happen only above this depth, since they require brittle rupture Marshak (2013) 268.

1.2

Rock strength: the Mohr circle

Not in Marshak, but verified against an ASBOG-recommended source: Hoek, Practical Rock Engineering, "Shear strength of discontinuities" Hoek Ch.4. The Mohr circle itself (graphing the stress state at a point, centered at (σ₁+σ₃)/2 with radius (σ₁−σ₃)/2) is standard construction not walked through step-by-step in that chapter, but the failure criterion it uses is directly sourced:

τp = c + σn·tan(φ)

where c = cohesion, defined by Hoek as the intercept on the shear-strength axis at zero normal stress, and φ = angle of friction, the slope of the peak-strength line Hoek Ch.4, Eq.1. Hoek distinguishes peak strength (initial resistance, includes true cohesion from any cementing material) from residual strength (after displacement, cohesion has dropped to zero: τ = σn·tan(φr), where φr is the residual friction angle) Hoek Ch.4. In rock mechanics, "cohesion" is often used loosely as a mathematical fitting term tied to surface roughness rather than true cementation — worth knowing since it's a common point of confusion Hoek Ch.4. The USBR Field Manual independently confirms the same equation in Coulomb form, S = C + N·tan(φ + i), where i is an added roughness angle USBR Vol.1, Ch.5, p.108.

A rock (or discontinuity) fails when the Mohr circle grows large enough to become tangent to this line; the tangent point's angle indicates the orientation of the fracture that forms. The standard result: fractures form at an acute angle to σ₁, roughly θ ≈ 45° − φ/2 from the maximum principal stress direction — why conjugate fracture sets typically form at less than 90° to each other, not exactly perpendicular.

Scope note: Hoek's chapter goes considerably deeper than FG needs — joint roughness coefficient (JRC), joint wall compressive strength (JCS), the Barton-Bandis criterion, scale corrections. That level of detail is PG/practicing-engineer territory. The equation and cohesion/friction-angle definitions above are the FG-relevant slice.

1.3

Brittle structures: joints, faults & orientation

Joints are fractures with no measurable offset; faults are fractures with displacement across them Marshak (2013) 266, 271. A planar structure's orientation is described by strike (the compass bearing of a horizontal line on the plane, relative to true north) and dip (the angle the plane slopes down from horizontal, measured perpendicular to strike) Marshak (2013) 272, Box 9.1.

Fault typeMotionTectonic setting
Strike-slip (left- or right-lateral)Sideways along strike, no vertical offset; steep to vertical dipTransform plate boundaries
Normal (dip-slip)Hanging wall slides down relative to footwallExtension/rifting — stretches crust
Reverse (dip-slip)Hanging wall slides up relative to footwallCompression/shortening; gentle-dip reverse fault = thrust fault
Oblique-slipDiagonal — a combination of strike-slip and dip-slipTranstensional/transpressional settings

Hanging wall = the block of rock above the fault surface; footwall = the block below it Marshak (2013) 273, Fig. 9.8. Left-lateral vs. right-lateral for strike-slip faults is determined by facing the fault and observing which way the far block moved Marshak (2013) 273. Independently corroborated, including the map-notation convention for strike/dip symbols (a short tick mark showing dip direction and angle off a strike line): Earle Ch.12, §12.4.

Original diagram — normal/reverse geometry follows Marshak (2013) 273, Fig. 9.8b; hanging wall moves relative to footwall, not the reverse.

Recognizing faults: displacement (offset layers), fault scarps (steps on the landscape from dip-slip/oblique-slip movement), slip lineations/striations (grooves or scratches on the fault surface recording slip direction) Marshak (2013) 273.

1.4

Ductile structures: folds & foliation

Fold anatomy: the hinge is the line of maximum curvature; the limbs are the two sides; the axial surface bisects the fold Marshak (2013) 275, Fig. 9.11.

Fold typeGeometry
AnticlineArch-shaped — limbs dip away from the hinge
SynclineTrough-shaped — limbs dip toward the hinge
MonoclineCarpet-over-a-stair-step shape — one steep limb between two flat ones
DomeOverturned-bowl fold — oldest rock in the center, bull's-eye outcrop pattern
BasinUpright-bowl fold — youngest rock in the center, also bull's-eye

Folds are also plunging (hinge tilted from horizontal) or nonplunging (hinge horizontal) Marshak (2013) 275.

Two formation mechanisms: flexural-slip folding (layers bend and slip past each other, like bending a deck of cards) and passive-flow folding (rock is soft enough to flow like plastic, different parts moving at different rates) Marshak (2013) 276. Folds form from end-on compression, from shear stress shifting one part of a layer over another, from layers draping over fault-related steps, or from basement-block uplift warping overlying sediment Marshak (2013) 276.

Tectonic foliation — planar fabric from deformation-driven alignment of platy/elongate minerals (distinct from bedding). Ties directly to your Category 2 metamorphism content: slaty cleavage, schistosity, and gneissic banding are all foliation types, just named by grain size/mineralogy Marshak (2013) 276.

1.5

Structural fabrics & map/cross-section interpretation

Verified in Marshak: slip lineations on fault surfaces (a linear fabric — grooves/striations recording slip direction, described by plunge and bearing) Marshak (2013) 273; tectonic foliation as a planar fabric Marshak (2013) 276; bull's-eye outcrop patterns for domes/basins as a basic form of map-pattern reading Marshak (2013) 275–276, 287.

Stereonets — verified via the USBR Field Manual. Orientation data (strike/dip, or trend/plunge for lineations) is plotted as points on an equal-area (Schmidt) or equal-angle (Wulff) net, then contoured to identify dominant fracture sets/fabric orientations USBR Vol.1, Ch.7, p.208–209, Fig. 7-1. Independently corroborated with a cleaner statement of the right-hand-rule convention: "When you are facing in the strike direction, the plane dips downward to your right" — equivalently, strike is always 90° counter-clockwise from dip direction Waldron & Snyder §1.2.

Structure contours, three-point problem & the rule of Vs — resolved. Found in Waldron & Snyder, Geological Structures: A Practical Introduction (University of Alberta / Open Education Alberta, open textbook, CC BY-NC), a source specifically dedicated to this technique that Marshak, the USBR manual, and Earle all lack:

  • Structure contour — a line of equal elevation on a buried (or once-present) geologic surface, exactly analogous to a topographic contour but drawn on a stratigraphic horizon, fault plane, or unconformity. Because they're lines of constant elevation, structure contours are automatically parallel to strike — they're sometimes just called strike lines Waldron & Snyder §1.2.
  • Dip from contour spacing — tan(dip) = contour interval ÷ contour spacing. Closely spaced contours = steep dip; widely spaced = gentle dip Waldron & Snyder §1.2.
  • Three-point problem — given three points A (highest), B (intermediate), C (lowest) on the same planar surface: connect A–C, find the point B′ on that line at B's elevation (by linear interpolation: distance AB′ = distance AC × (a−b)/(a−c), using elevations a, b, c), then connect B to B′ — that line is a structure contour, and its orientation is the strike. Repeat/extrapolate to get contours at round-number elevations Waldron & Snyder, Lab 2. (This matches what was drafted here previously from general reasoning — confirmed correct, not corrected.)
  • Rule of Vs, exact statement — "the V-shape formed as a trace crosses a river valley points in the direction of dip... The only exception occurs when the dip is in the same direction as the slope of the valley, but gentler than the gradient of the river; then the V-shapes point up-dip." Horizontal beds: trace parallels topographic contours (no V). Vertical beds: straight trace, ignores topography entirely Waldron & Snyder §1.2. This is the one sub-point flagged last pass as too risky to state from memory — now properly sourced.

Rose diagrams — resolved, with a caveat on domain fit. Andrews, J.T. (1971), Techniques of Till Fabric Analysis, British Geomorphological Research Group Technical Bulletin No. 6 (via University of Regina) Andrews 1971, describes the standard construction: a rose diagram portrays the number or proportion of measurements (pebble/fracture/lineation orientations) falling into azimuthal class intervals, plotted as a circular histogram. Two real caveats worth keeping: (1) the visual pattern is sensitive to class-interval width and starting azimuth — the same dataset binned differently can look meaningfully different; (2) for axial data with no directional sense (a fracture strikes the same whether read as 040° or 220°), frequencies are conventionally repeated through 180°, which exaggerates the apparent strength of the fabric compared to a true 360° distribution. Domain-fit note: this source describes rose diagrams for glacial till-clast orientation, not fracture/joint orientation — the graphing technique is identical either way, but it's a 1971 technical bulletin on a different application than ASBOG's E-3.2 fabric-analysis context, so treat the construction/caveats as verified and the specific example (till fabric) as illustrative only.

02 / Tectonics

Plate boundaries, driving forces, orogeny, volcanism

2.1

Plate tectonics fundamentals

Three plate boundary types Marshak (2013) 51–61, §2.7–2.9:

BoundaryMotionKey features
DivergentTwo plates move apartMid-ocean ridge, sea-floor spreading, new oceanic crust (gabbro at depth, basalt/pillow lavas at surface), black smokers, ridge-parallel normal faults
ConvergentPlates move together; at least one is oceanic and subductsDeep trench, volcanic arc (island arc if oceanic-oceanic, continental arc if oceanic-continental), accretionary prism
TransformPlates slide sideways past each otherLarge strike-slip fault, no new crust created or destroyed; links ridge segments (fracture zones)

Continental crust cannot subduct (too buoyant) — when two continents converge, the intervening ocean basin closes and the continents themselves collide Marshak (2013) 61, §2.11.

Original diagram — boundary geometry follows Marshak's "Theory of Plate Tectonics" spread Marshak (2013) 64–65, Geology at a Glance

Driving forces — three mechanisms contribute, not a single cause Marshak (2013) 65–66, §2.12:

  • Mantle convection — broad-scale upwelling/downwelling in the asthenosphere may exert drag on the base of plates, though the match between convection patterns and plate boundaries isn't exact
  • Ridge-push force — mid-ocean ridge lithosphere sits at higher elevation than adjacent abyssal plains; gravity causes this elevated mass to push laterally away from the ridge
  • Slab-pull force — subducting lithosphere older than ~10 Ma is denser than the asthenosphere it sinks into, so it pulls the rest of the plate along behind it, like an anchor line

Slab-pull is generally regarded as the dominant force among the three.

2.2

Convergent settings & mountain building

Mountains form primarily via three settings, all linear because the driving boundaries/rifts are linear Marshak (2013) 279:

  • Subduction-related orogens (e.g., the Andes) — compression on the overriding plate produces fold-thrust belts; thrust slices stack and fold, merging into a subhorizontal detachment at depth. Buoyant crustal fragments (volcanic arcs, oceanic plateaus, microcontinents) that arrive at the trench are too buoyant to subduct and instead "suture" onto the margin — called accretion; offshore, such a fragment is an exotic terrane, once attached it's an accreted terrane. Orogens built by repeated accretion are accretionary orogens (the western North American Cordillera is a real-world example) Marshak (2013) 279–280.
  • Continental collision (e.g., Himalayas, Alps, the final Appalachian collision with Africa ~300 Ma) — once the intervening ocean fully subducts, continents collide directly; intense compression produces fold-thrust belts at the margins Marshak (2013) 281.
  • Continental rifting — the reverse process; stretching and thinning of the lithosphere allows asthenosphere to rise, and the crust may eventually break to form a new mid-ocean ridge.

Cratons are the stable, ancient interiors of continents; they contain shields (exposed Precambrian basement) and platforms (basement covered by a thin veneer of flat-lying sediment). Broad, gentle regional domes and basins — not the tight folds of an orogen — form within platforms Marshak (2013) 287–288.

Isostasy — the principle that crust "floats" on the mantle in gravitational equilibrium; thickened crust (from collision) rises to higher elevation, and removal of dense lithospheric mantle root material (delamination) can drive further uplift after collision ends, analogous to a ship rising as ballast is removed Marshak (2013) 285–287.

03 / Seismology

Earthquake mechanism, waves, hazard & the deep interior

3.1

Earthquake mechanism

Marshak's actual terms — not "elastic rebound" alone: elastic-rebound theory and stick-slip behavior Marshak (2013) 221. During fault formation, rock bends elastically as stress builds, storing strain energy. When stress overcomes friction on the fault, the rock ruptures suddenly, the stored elastic strain is released, and the rock snaps back toward its unstrained shape — this vibration is the earthquake. Faults don't creep continuously; they move in sudden increments (stick-slip), each increment a separate earthquake.

Key terms: focus (a.k.a. hypocenter) = the actual point of rupture at depth; epicenter = the point on the surface directly above the focus Marshak (2013) 219. Foreshocks precede the main rupture, aftershocks follow it as the fault zone adjusts Marshak (2013) 221.

3.2

Seismic waves, measurement & magnitude

Wave typeCategoryMotionSpeed
P-wave (compressional)Body wavePush-pull, parallel to travel directionFastest — arrives first
S-wave (shear)Body waveSide-to-side, perpendicular to travel direction; cannot travel through liquidSlower than P
L-wave (Love)Surface waveGround moves back and forth horizontallySlower still, largest amplitude
R-wave (Rayleigh)Surface waveGround moves up and down (rolling motion)Similar to L-wave

A seismometer records ground motion as a seismogram; the arrival-time gap between P- and S-waves (the S–P interval) is used to calculate distance to the epicenter, and triangulating from three or more stations locates it Marshak (2013) 225–227.

Magnitude vs. intensity — these measure fundamentally different things Marshak (2013) 228–230:

  • Magnitude (Richter/local magnitude ML, or moment magnitude MW) — an objective, instrument-based measure of the energy released. ML is distance-limited and less accurate for large or distant earthquakes; MW is the modern standard, calculated from wave amplitudes, fault-plane dimensions, and displacement. Largest ever recorded: 1960 Chile, MW 9.5.
  • Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) — a subjective scale based on observed damage/felt effects at a specific location; varies by distance from epicenter and local ground conditions, unlike magnitude, which is a single number for the whole event.

All magnitude scales are logarithmic: +1 magnitude unit = 10× the ground-motion amplitude and roughly 32× the energy release. A magnitude 8 quake releases about 1,000,000× more energy than a magnitude 4 Marshak (2013) 229.

3.3

Distribution, hazard, prediction & the deep interior

Most earthquakes cluster in seismic belts, largely coincident with plate boundaries; intraplate earthquakes occur within plate interiors, away from boundaries, and are generally less understood Marshak (2013) 231, 236. At subduction zones, earthquake foci define an inclined plane dipping into the mantle along the downgoing slab — the Wadati-Benioff zone Marshak (2013) 233.

Hazards: ground shaking, landslides, sediment liquefaction (saturated loose sediment temporarily behaves like a fluid under shaking), fire, and tsunamis Marshak (2013) 236–240.

Prediction: seismologists can identify seismic zones as more likely and estimate recurrence intervals for large earthquakes, but precise time/place prediction remains out of reach Marshak (2013) 245. Mitigation instead relies on earthquake engineering (retrofitting, building codes) and zoning Marshak (2013) 247–248.

Earth's interior, from seismic evidence Marshak (2013) 253–259, Interlude D, independently corroborated (same depths for Moho, CMB, and shadow zones) Earle Ch.9, §9.1:

Boundary/featureDepthEvidence
Moho (crust–mantle)7–70 km (varies by location)Seismic-velocity discontinuity
Core–mantle boundary2,900 kmP-wave shadow zone (103°–143° from epicenter) — refraction/absorption pattern implies a dense, different-composition core
Outer core / inner core boundary5,155 kmS-wave shadow zone — S-waves don't propagate through the outer core at all, meaning it's liquid
Earth's center6,371 km—

Continental crust averages 35–40 km thick, roughly 4–5× thicker than oceanic crust. The technique underlying all of this — seismic reflection and refraction — is the same one used at Interlude D scale (whole-Earth) and at engineering-site scale (Domain G territory); the physics doesn't change, only the depth of investigation.

3.4

Paleoseismic history

Paleoseismology exploration methods and OSL specifically remain unconfirmed — checked five sources now (Marshak, USBR, Earle, Waldron & Snyder, White), zero hits for "paleoseismology," "OSL," or "thermoluminescence" in any of them. Trenching methodology and OSL description below are standard content, no page reference exists for those two pieces.
  • Exploration methods — trenching across a fault trace to expose offset stratigraphy; each buried, offset soil horizon or sand blow (liquefaction feature) can represent one prehistoric rupture event, allowing a recurrence interval to be estimated from the vertical stack of events. Gap
  • Absolute age determination — ¹⁴C (radiocarbon) dating of organic material in offset/buried horizons (already in your Category 2 isotope table — same technique, applied here to constrain rupture timing rather than rock age).
  • Absolute age determination — cosmogenic nuclide exposure dating, newly verified. White's Isotope Geochemistry §4.1.5.3 covers ³⁶Cl (and ²⁶Al) exposure dating directly, with a worked paleoseismology-adjacent example: Phillips et al. (1990) dated moraine boulders at Bloody Canyon, eastern Sierra Nevada, by measuring ³⁶Cl built up in the rock surface since exposure to cosmic rays — the same logic used to date fault-scarp surfaces once cosmogenic-nuclide production rates are known for the site's latitude, elevation, and rock chemistry (Cl, K, Ca content) White §4.1.5.3. Caveats the source itself raises: inherited nuclides from prior exposure, preferential leaching, surface erosion, and snow shielding all bias ages young, so the maximum age in a scatter of results is generally preferred. This isn't OSL, but it's the same category of problem (dating a geomorphic surface's exposure history) and is a real, standard technique — useful bonus content even though it doesn't close the specific OSL request.
  • Absolute age determination — OSL (optically stimulated luminescence) dates the last time quartz/feldspar sediment grains were exposed to sunlight, useful where no datable organic material is present — common in fault-scarp colluvium and alluvial fan deposits. Gap — not found in any of the five sources checked; would need a dedicated Quaternary geochronology/luminescence-dating source.
  • Relative age determination — standard cross-cutting relationships (a fault that offsets a unit is younger than that unit; a unit that buries/onlaps a fault scarp without being offset postdates the most recent rupture) — this part is just applying principles you already know from stratigraphy to a fault-trench context.
