01 / Ore & Mineral Systems

H-1

1.1

Ore Minerals & Economic Use

An ore mineral (economic mineral) contains a metal in high enough concentration, in an easily extractable form, to be worth mining Marshak p.376. Ore is rock containing native metals or a sufficient concentration of ore minerals to profitably mine; the grade of an ore is the concentration of the useful metal, and whether a given grade is worth mining depends on current metal price Marshak p.376. Galena (PbS) is ~50% lead and is the principal lead ore mineral; iron is obtained mainly from hematite and magnetite; copper comes from a variety of sulfide and carbonate minerals (chalcopyrite, azurite, malachite), none of which look metallic Marshak pp.375–376. Many ore minerals are sulfides (metal + sulfur) or oxides (metal + oxygen).

1.2

Magmatic Deposits

When magma cools, dense sulfide minerals crystallize early and settle to the floor of the magma chamber, concentrating as a magmatic deposit — often called a massive sulfide deposit once the magma solidifies around it Marshak pp.376–377. Where ore minerals instead fill fractures in pre-existing rock, the result is a vein deposit; where they disperse through an intrusion itself, it is a disseminated deposit — both typically hydrothermal in origin (see 1.3) Marshak p.377.

Pegmatites are very coarse-grained igneous rocks that crystallize from water-rich residual melt late in cooling; many gemstones (emerald, beryl) form this way, and pegmatites are also a major lithium/rare-element ore type Marshak p.85. Kimberlites are ultramafic igneous rocks that carry diamonds up from ~150 km depth during rapid, rifting-related ascent from the mantle; named for Kimberley, South Africa, where the type locality was identified Marshak pp.84–85.

Carbonatites are intrusive, carbonate-mineral-rich igneous rocks (commonly with apatite, magnetite, barite, fluorite) that host economic or anomalous concentrations of rare earth elements, phosphorus, niobium, uranium, thorium, and other incompatible elements; they occur as central plugs in zoned alkalic intrusive complexes or as dikes, sills, breccias, and veins. Type examples include Mountain Pass, California (a major U.S. REE mine) and Oka, Quebec.

Note: H-1.2.1 groups pegmatites, kimberlites, and carbonatites under "magmatic," though all three are more precisely late- or post-magmatic (residual-melt or carbonate-melt) processes rather than simple crystal settling — worth knowing the distinction if a question probes mechanism rather than just naming the deposit type.
1.3

Hydrothermal Deposits

Hydrothermal activity — circulation of hot-water solutions through or around a cooling intrusion — dissolves metal ions, which precipitate as ore minerals when the fluid hits lower pressure, lower temperature, or a different chemistry, filling fractures (vein deposits) or dispersing through the host rock (disseminated deposits) Marshak p.377.

Porphyry copper deposits are large, low-grade (commonly ~0.4–1% Cu) deposits of disseminated and stockwork-hosted copper (with Mo, Au, Ag) associated with felsic-to-intermediate porphyritic intrusions at convergent margins; they supply roughly 60% of world copper production despite their low grade, because of their enormous size (hundreds of millions to billions of tonnes) — Bingham Canyon, Utah, is the classic North American example USGS SIR 2010–5070–B. Epithermal deposits form at shallow crustal levels from the same magmatic-hydrothermal systems that produce porphyry deposits, typically as gold- and silver-bearing quartz veins.

Volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits form where hydrothermal fluids venting from submarine volcanoes along mid-ocean ridges meet cold seawater; dissolved metals and sulfur precipitate instantly as a plume of fine sulfide crystals ("black smokers"), which settle to build a mound of massive sulfide ore around the vent Marshak pp.377–378. Modern VMS deposits are actively forming today at spreading centers and back-arc basins; ancient examples (Cyprus-type, Kuroko-type) supply much of the world's Cu-Zn-Pb-Au-Ag from volcanic-hosted sulfide sources.

Gap: "epigenic/epigenetic" as a formal deposit-type label (as opposed to "epithermal") is not independently defined in any source checked; treat it as the general term for any deposit formed after its host rock, distinct from "syngenetic" (formed with the host) — a definitional distinction rather than a named deposit type. Similarly, geothermal brines as an ore-forming process (vs. an energy source, see H-2.3) are noted in the KB but not treated in depth here; the mechanism is essentially the same hydrothermal precipitation described above, applied to actively circulating geothermal systems (e.g., Salton Sea brines, a known source of dissolved Li, Zn, Mn).
1.4

Sedimentary Ore Deposits

Placer deposits form where erosion of a native-metal-bearing rock produces a mix of sand and metal grains; moving water winnows away the lighter quartz/feldspar grains while dense metal grains (gold, platinum, cassiterite) concentrate in stream sediment — the mechanism behind gold panning Marshak pp.378–379.

Banded iron formations (BIF) are Precambrian (mostly 2.4–1.8 Ga) chemical sedimentary deposits of alternating iron-oxide (magnetite/hematite) and iron-poor chert (jasper) layers. They record the rise of atmospheric/oceanic oxygen from early photosynthesis: as free oxygen appeared, dissolved ferrous iron oxidized to insoluble ferric iron and precipitated on the sea floor; once the ocean's dissolved iron was drawn down (~1.8 Ga), BIF deposition essentially stopped Marshak p.378.

Residual mineral deposits (laterites) form by extreme chemical weathering in warm, wet climates: rainwater leaches soluble elements out of soil, leaving behind insoluble residues concentrated in iron or aluminum. Most mined aluminum ore is bauxite, a laterite formed by intense leaching of aluminum-silicate rocks such as granite Marshak p.378; the same leaching process, described generally in the soil-formation literature, produces the hard, brick-red laterite soil horizon Marshak p.157.

Evaporite deposits (gypsum, halite, sylvite/potash) precipitate as restricted seawater or saline lake water evaporates and successive minerals reach saturation; treated in this guide primarily as nonmetallic resources (see 4.2), though evaporite basins are also the classic host for MVT-type metal deposits (see 1.6) Marshak p.381.

Phosphorites are sedimentary rocks whose main mineral is francolite (carbonate-fluorapatite); P₂O₅ content exceeds 18% (commonly reaching values as high as 37%), versus under 1% in ordinary sedimentary rock Kolodny 2009, pp.775–776. Formation follows the Kazakov (1937) model, still the basis of modern theory: oceanic upwelling brings cold, phosphate-rich deep water onto the continental shelf; as it warms, CO₂ is released and francolite precipitates interstitially in the sediment, a few centimeters below the sediment–water interface. High-grade deposits additionally require mechanical reworking/winnowing to concentrate the francolite Kolodny 2009, p.778. Phosphorites are commonly associated with chert and black shale (the "P–Si–C" trinity) and occur as bedded, bioclastic, nodular, or pebble-bed textures; the Permian Phosphoria Formation (western U.S.) and the Cretaceous–Eocene southern Tethys belt are classic examples Kolodny 2009, p.776. Phosphorites supply about 85% of world phosphate consumption, mostly for fertilizer, and are increasingly studied as an unconventional rare-earth-element resource Kolodny 2009, p.779; Dar et al. 2025.

1.5

Metamorphic Deposits: Skarn

A skarn deposit forms where a felsic to intermediate pluton intrudes carbonate rock (limestone or dolostone); heat and hydrothermal fluids from the intrusion drive contact metamorphism and metasomatism, replacing the carbonate with calc-silicate minerals (garnet, pyroxene) and precipitating ore minerals within this altered zone USGS Bulletin 1693 (Cox & Theodore 1986). Copper skarns are the most common economically; the mineralogy is typically chalcopyrite + pyrite ± magnetite ± bornite, with coarse granoblastic texture and bladed pyroxenes. Skarns commonly occur genetically alongside porphyry copper systems, since both derive from the same intrusion-driven hydrothermal system — one hosted in the intrusion/its halo (porphyry), the other in adjacent carbonate country rock (skarn) USGS Bulletin 1693.

1.6

Meteoric/Groundwater Deposits

Mississippi Valley-type (MVT) deposits form when rainwater entering one margin of a large sedimentary basin flows as groundwater down to the basin's hottest, deepest part — dissolving lead and zinc along the way — then flows back up and out at the opposite margin, precipitating Pb-Zn sulfide ore minerals in dolomite as it cools; named for classic occurrences in the Mississippi Valley region of the central U.S. Marshak p.379.

Roll-front uranium deposits form by the same basic groundwater-redox mechanism described (without the "roll front" name) in Marshak's treatment of uranium ore genesis: oxidizing, uranium-bearing groundwater moves through a permeable sandstone aquifer until it reaches a reducing environment (organic matter, pyrite, or hydrocarbon-derived H₂S), where U(VI) is reduced to insoluble U(IV) and precipitates Marshak pp.367–368. In cross-section the ore body forms a crescent- or roll-shaped front at the oxidation–reduction boundary within the sandstone — the industry-standard model for locating sandstone-hosted uranium, first recognized in Wyoming in the early 1960s Wyoming State Geological Survey. A comparable system in the South Texas Uranium Region shows deposits forming at the margin of altered (oxidized) sandstone tongues encroaching into pyrite-bearing reduced sandstone, with reductant commonly supplied by H₂S migrating up-fault from deeper hydrocarbon accumulations USGS Texas Coastal Plain roll-front model.

02 / Geologic Energy Resources

H-2

2.1

Coal Deposits

Coal forms from plant debris deposited and buried in oxygen-poor swamp environments, which prevents decay; compaction converts the debris to peat (~50% carbon). Deep burial (4–10 km) heats the peat, driving off hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur as gas and progressively concentrating carbon: peat → lignite (~50% C) → bituminous (~70% C, ~100–200°C) → anthracite (~90% C, ~200–300°C) Marshak pp.364–365. Rising carbon concentration with burial/temperature is called increasing coal rank. Anthracite requires unusually high temperature, typically reached only near mountain belts where thrust faulting buries coal-bearing strata to 8–10 km Marshak p.365. Coal occurs as beds interlayered with other sedimentary rock; near-surface seams (within ~100 m) are strip-mined, deeper seams require underground mining Marshak pp.365–366. Coalbed methane is natural gas generated during coalification and trapped within the coal; it is produced by drilling and pumping down groundwater to release pressure, rather than by mining Marshak p.367.

2.2

Oil & Gas — Traditional Systems

A complete hydrocarbon system requires a source rock (organic-rich black shale formed from buried plankton in oxygen-poor water), sufficient burial temperature (the oil window, roughly 90–160°C, above which oil cracks to gas and above ~225–250°C all hydrogen is lost to graphite), a reservoir rock with adequate porosity and permeability for oil/gas to migrate into and be extracted, and a trap — a seal rock (shale, salt, unfractured limestone) arranged in a geometry that concentrates the buoyantly rising hydrocarbons Marshak pp.355–357. Four classic trap geometries: anticline (oil collects at the crest of an upward fold), fault trap (impermeable fault gouge or juxtaposed impermeable rock blocks migration), salt-dome trap (buoyant salt rising through strata bends up adjacent reservoir beds), and stratigraphic trap (a tilted reservoir bed pinches out between impermeable layers) Marshak p.359. Exploration uses geologic mapping plus seismic-reflection profiling (a vibrating truck or explosive source generates seismic waves that reflect off rock-layer contacts, imaging subsurface structure) Marshak pp.357–358. Standard rotary drilling with circulating drilling mud (which cools the bit and counters formation pressure to prevent blowouts) accesses the reservoir; directional drilling can steer the drill bit to hit a target only ~15 cm wide from kilometers away Marshak pp.360–361.

2.3

Oil & Gas — Unconventional Systems

Shale gas is extracted directly from low-permeability source rock (rather than a separate reservoir rock) using directional drilling combined with hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") — pumping a high-pressure water/chemical/sand mixture to create and prop open new fractures, dramatically increasing permeability; the Marcellus Shale of the northeastern U.S. is the flagship example Marshak pp.361–363. Tar sand (oil sand) contains very viscous bitumen ("heavy oil") that cannot be pumped; near-surface deposits are strip-mined and heated to extract the oil (Alberta, Canada, is the largest deposit). Oil shale is organic shale that never reached oil-window temperatures and still contains kerogen (15–30% by weight defines "oil shale"); producing liquid oil requires heating the rock to ~500°C to break down the kerogen Marshak pp.362–363.

2.4

Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy exploits Earth's internal heat (partly primordial, partly from radioactive decay), which heats groundwater; where the geothermal gradient is steep, high temperatures occur at shallow depth. Hot groundwater can be pumped directly for heating, or — where it flashes to steam on decompression at the surface — used to drive turbines for electricity (major applications in volcanically active areas like Iceland and New Zealand) Marshak p.369. Shallow geothermal heat pumps, which exploit the near-constant temperature of ground at ~6 m depth for heating/cooling, are feasible almost anywhere, unlike deep hydrothermal systems.

2.5

Nuclear Energy / Uranium Mineral Systems

Nuclear power plants generate heat via controlled fission of ²³⁵U fuel rods; released neutrons trigger a self-sustaining chain reaction, and control rods (which absorb neutrons) regulate the reaction rate to prevent meltdown Marshak p.367. Because natural uranium is only ~0.7% ²³⁵U (the rest ²³⁸U), reactor fuel requires enrichment.

Uranium originates from supernova nucleosynthesis and rises in granitic magma; hot hydrothermal water circulating through a cooled pluton dissolves uranium and precipitates it as pitchblende (UO₂) in veins. Weathering and erosion of uranium-bearing plutons can concentrate uranium further as placer deposits, or groundwater can dissolve and re-precipitate uranium in sedimentary host rock as it moves and encounters a reducing environment — the mechanism underlying roll-front deposits (see 1.6) Marshak pp.367–368.

03 / Prospecting & Exploration Techniques

H-3

3.1

Geophysical Methods

Gravity method: ground-based gravimeters detect anomalies from subsurface density contrasts; high-density ore (chromite, hematite, barite) yields gravity highs, low-density targets (halite, weathered kimberlite) yield gravity lows. Gravity and magnetic methods detect only lateral density/magnetization contrasts, whereas electrical and seismic methods can also resolve vertical contrasts USGS OFR 95-831, pp.19–20.

Magnetic method (including aeromagnetic surveys): exploits variation in magnetic mineralogy (magnetite, titanomagnetite, pyrrhotite); surveys can be ground-based or airborne (aeromagnetic). Magnetic anomalies often mark zones of hydrothermal alteration around ore deposits, and can directly detect iron ore (magnetite, BIF) USGS OFR 95-831, pp.20–21.

Gamma-ray (radiometric) methods: scintillometry/spectrometry detects natural potassium, uranium, and thorium; direct-detection method of choice for uranium exploration USGS OFR 95-831, p.21.

Seismic methods: reflection profiling provides fine structural detail (the standard method in oil/gas exploration, see 2.2); refraction provides precise depth-to-contact estimates and has been used to map low-velocity alluvial deposits (e.g., placer gold, tin, sand/gravel) USGS OFR 95-831, p.22.

Electrical methods comprise five classes: direct-current resistivity (rock resistivity reflects pore-water content; massive sulfide is a direct low-resistivity target); electromagnetic (EM) methods (induce current via alternating magnetic fields, traditionally used to find low-resistivity massive sulfide, airborne EM screens large areas); mise-a-la-masse (maps a known conductive body's extent by energizing it directly); induced polarization (IP) (detects disseminated sulfide/clay minerals via polarizable pore-surface response — IP is the standard method for disseminated ore, where DC resistivity works better on massive sulfide); and self-potential (detects natural electrical fields from ongoing sulfide oxidation or fluid/heat flow) USGS OFR 95-831, pp.22–24.

Remote sensing as a geophysical tool compares surface spectral reflectance to known mineral signatures, mapping iron-oxide, silica, and clay alteration zones over broad areas — the low-resolution ancestor of the airborne hyperspectral techniques in 3.4 USGS OFR 95-831, p.25.

3.2

Geochemical Methods

Soil and rock sampling for anomalous metal content, plus analysis of plants (which absorb metals through roots), are standard reconnaissance techniques once a geologically favorable region has been identified Marshak p.379. Stream-sediment sampling is the most widely used and most successful geochemical exploration technique, since a single sample integrates the weathering/erosion signal of an entire upstream catchment; sample media also include glacial till, soil gas (useful in arid terrain, sampling mercury vapor, H₂S, and other gases related to base-metal deposits), and hydrogeochemical sampling of spring, lake, stream, and well waters and their precipitates USGS PP 907-B. Orientation studies — determining the best sample medium and soil horizon for a given area before committing to a full survey — are standard practice USGS PP 907-B.

3.3

Rotary drilling with circulating drilling mud is standard for oil/gas and can also be directional (steerable to hit a specific target) Marshak pp.360–361. In mineral exploration specifically, three techniques dominate:

3.4

Remote Sensing & GIS

Geologic maps are the foundational data layer for any exploration program (see 2.2 for their oil-exploration use) Marshak p.357. LiDAR produces a 3D point cloud by timing laser-pulse round trips (R = c·Δt/2); filtering the "highest-hit" surface (first return — canopy, structures) down to the "bare-earth" surface (last return — ground only) reveals subtle terrain features — historic channels, landslides, alluvial fans — invisible under vegetation in conventional aerial photography USGS OFR 2010-1335 (Roberts). Airborne LiDAR offers three advantages over photogrammetry: it is a direct 3D measurement rather than an indirect derivation, it partially penetrates vegetation canopy, and as an active sensor it is largely independent of lighting/weather Sun et al. 2022, Frontiers in Earth Science.

Hyperspectral and multispectral imaging (airborne sensors with hundreds of narrow spectral bands, e.g. AVIRIS) discriminate rock and alteration minerals by comparing pixel spectra against reference mineral libraries, mapping hydrothermal alteration zones (kaolinite, chlorite, hematite, etc.) over broad, often inaccessible terrain — used in USGS mineral-exploration surveys of Alaska among other applications.

Note: GIS itself (vector/raster data models, geodatabases linking spatial features to attribute tables) is treated in your Domain A materials and is not duplicated here — the KB lists it under this same H-3.4 item alongside remote sensing.
3.5

Petrophysical Methods

Petrophysical properties — bulk density, grain density, porosity, magnetic susceptibility, permeability — are measured directly on rock specimens to calibrate and interpret geophysical surveys (3.1). A USGS compilation of 182 igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock specimens measured dry bulk density and grain density (by both direct measurement and the Archimedes method), calculated total, water-accessible, and helium-accessible porosity from the density data, and measured magnetic susceptibility, permeability, and streaming potential on most samples — finding, for example, that sedimentary rocks had the highest percentage of occluded porosity per rock volume, while metamorphic rocks had the highest percentage of occluded porosity relative to total porosity USGS OFR 83-736 (Johnson 1983). Ore and gangue minerals themselves have distinctive physical-property signatures (density, magnetic susceptibility, electrical conductivity) that geophysical surveys are tuned to detect — e.g., massive sulfide minerals have specific gravity of 4.0–7.5 g/cm³, sharply contrasting with typical country rock USGS SIR 2010–5070–C, Table 7-2.

3.6

Analytical Methods

Fire assay is the classical total-decomposition method for determining precious-metal content: a pulverized sample is fused with lead-oxide flux at high temperature, collecting gold/silver/platinum-group metals into a lead button, which is then cupelled (oxidized in a porous cup) to leave a doré bead of the precious metals for weighing or further instrumental analysis USGS Bulletin 1445. Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) dissolves the sample (commonly in hot nitric acid, with other acid combinations for specific elements) and measures light absorption by free atoms of the target element in a flame or graphite furnace USGS Bulletin 1289. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) methods (ICP-AES/ICP-MS) ionize a dissolved sample in an argon plasma for simultaneous multi-element analysis, offering much lower detection limits than AAS. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) excites a solid or powdered sample with X-rays and measures the characteristic fluorescent X-rays emitted by each element, allowing rapid, largely non-destructive multi-element analysis without full dissolution. The electron microprobe focuses a beam of electrons on a small part of a mineral grain (in polished thin section) to determine its precise chemical composition in place, without destroying grain-scale textural context Marshak Interlude A, p.93.

04 / Development & Mining Methods

H-4

4.1

Resource & Reserve Assessment

The U.S. Bureau of Mines/USGS classification system (the "McKelvey diagram") divides total mineral endowment into identified resources (location, grade, and quality known or estimable from geologic evidence and engineering measurement) and undiscovered resources (surmised to exist from broad geologic evidence only), each further subdivided by economic feasibility. A reserve is the portion of an identified resource that can be economically and legally extracted at the time of determination — reserves are therefore not fixed: they shrink as ore is mined and grow as new deposits are found or as price/technology changes make previously uneconomic material minable USGS Bulletin 1450-A. Within identified resources, measured tonnage/grade is computed from closely spaced sample data with high confidence; indicated and inferred categories represent progressively lower sampling density and confidence USGS Bulletin 1450-A.

4.2

Quality & Quantity Evaluation

Grade is the concentration of a useful metal or mineral in ore; the higher the grade, the higher-quality the ore. Whether a given grade is worth mining is a function of current market price, not a fixed geologic threshold — the same deposit can move from waste to ore (or back) purely because of price change, without any change in the rock itself Marshak p.376. Quantity is expressed as tonnage of ore and is central to both the McKelvey classification (4.1) and to economic evaluation (4.3).

4.3

Economic Evaluation & Cutoff Grade

The cutoff grade is the minimum grade of mineralization, set by reference to current economic factors (metal price, mining/processing cost), above which material is classed as ore and included in reserve calculations, and below which it is treated as waste. An internal cutoff grade applies to blocks of mineralization within the confines of a planned pit; an external cutoff grade controls the outer design limits of the pit or underground workings general mining-economics terminology, cross-checked across standard industry definitions. Cutoff grade is not fixed: it rises when metal prices fall or costs increase, and falls when prices rise — directly linking the economic-evaluation concept in this item to the grade/tonnage relationship in 4.1–4.2.

4.4

Surface Mining Methods

Open-pit mining is used where ore lies within roughly 100 m of the surface: workers drill and blast the bedrock into manageable blocks, front-end loaders move ore to a crusher and waste rock ("tailings") to a tailings pile; benched, stepped pit walls maintain slope stability Marshak pp.379–380. Strip mining is the coal-specific surface method: a dragline shovel strips overburden to expose a shallow (within ~100 m) coal seam, smaller shovels then extract the coal Marshak pp.365–366. Quarrying produces dimension stone (cut in intact blocks/slabs using wire saws, thermal lances, or abrasive water jets) or crushed stone (blasted and mechanically crushed for aggregate/cement) — note that a quarry provides stone, while a mine provides ore Marshak pp.380–381.

4.5

Underground Mining Methods

Where ore lies deeper than ~100 m, miners sink a vertical shaft (with elevator/hoist) or drive a horizontal tunnel (adit) into a mountainside, then excavate a maze of tunnels into the ore body by drilling and blasting, leaving rock pillars to support the roof Marshak p.379. Underground coal mining uses large grinding machines to chew coal from the seam onto a conveyor; hazards include roof collapse and methane accumulation from coalification, which can trigger explosions Marshak p.366.

05 / Environmental, Health/Safety & Reclamation

H-5 / H-6

5.1

Geological Aspects Affecting Human Health & Environment

Underground coal mining exposes workers to methane-explosion risk and, without filtered breathing protection, to black lung disease from coal-dust inhalation Marshak p.366. Sulfide-mineral oxidation (mining or coal waste containing pyrite) produces acid mine drainage/acid rock drainage — dilute sulfuric acid that mobilizes toxic metals (copper, zinc, lead) into surface water, harming aquatic life Marshak pp.372–373, 382–383. Burning coal releases sulfur (as SO₂, forming acid rain) and, universally, CO₂ Marshak p.373.

The same geophysical techniques used in exploration (3.1) apply directly to geoenvironmental site characterization: gamma-ray spectrometry assesses radiation dose/radon potential where U/K are enriched near ore or waste; direct-current resistivity and electromagnetic surveys trace acid-mine-drainage plumes (which become less conductive as they are buffered/neutralized downstream); induced polarization identifies disseminated sulfide oxidation in waste dumps; and magnetometer surveys can trace burned-out, pyrite-bearing coal seams, since pyrite oxidizes to magnetic magnetite during combustion USGS OFR 95-831, pp.21, 24–25.

5.2

Extraction Techniques

Extraction-technique content overlaps directly with the surface and underground mining methods described in 4.4–4.5; the KB separates this item to emphasize the environmental/safety lens on the same physical operations rather than introducing new techniques.

5.3

Waste Management

Tailings (waste rock without economic ore-mineral content) accumulate in tailings piles that can grow to 200 m high and kilometers long; lacking soil, they resist revegetation and, since ore minerals are commonly sulfides, generate acid mine runoff when exposed to rain and oxygen — sometimes deliberately doused with acidic solution by operators to leach out residual metal, which can then escape into the environment Marshak pp.382–383. Nuclear waste requires far longer-term management: some radioactive material decays within decades, but some remains dangerous for thousands of years or more, and hot waste must be actively cooled; ideal disposal seals waste in containers designed to outlast the decay period, in a location isolated from groundwater and surface contact — a genuinely difficult siting problem Marshak p.369. Waste-pile thickness and structures that channel contaminated groundwater flow can themselves be characterized geophysically (electromagnetic/DC resistivity soundings, seismic refraction/reflection, ground-penetrating radar) USGS OFR 95-831, p.25.

5.4

Reclamation & Restoration

Modern strip-mine practice separates and preserves topsoil ahead of the dragline; once coal is removed, the pit is backfilled with the stripped overburden and capped with the saved soil to allow revegetation — though in hilly terrain, "mountaintop removal" (dumping blasted overburden into adjacent valleys) causes more permanent landscape disruption Marshak p.366. More broadly, "efforts to reclaim mining spoils" and new, less environmentally damaging extraction technologies are ongoing industry and regulatory priorities Marshak p.383.

The one substantial federal reclamation framework is the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 (SMCRA), administered by the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement (OSMRE), Dept. of the Interior. SMCRA requires a reclamation plan and a bond (sufficient to cover reclamation cost) before permitting, mandates restoring mined land to a condition capable of supporting its prior (or better) use, and funds an Abandoned Mine Land program — via a per-ton fee on coal production — to remediate pre-1977 mine sites with no other legal responsible party.

Gap/scope flag: SMCRA applies specifically to coal mining. Hardrock/metal-mine reclamation in the U.S. has no directly equivalent comprehensive federal statute — it is regulated mainly at the state level, supplemented by BLM regulations (43 CFR 3809) on federal land and by general environmental statutes (Clean Water Act, RCRA) where applicable. If a question specifies a metal mine rather than a coal mine, do not assume SMCRA governs it.
