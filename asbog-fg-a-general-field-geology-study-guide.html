01 / Earth Systems & Information Sources

A-1 & A-2

1.1

Earth Systems and Processes

Earth's five interacting components that make up the "Earth System" M p.21: atmosphere (gaseous envelope), hydrosphere (surface/near-surface water), biosphere (life), lithosphere (outer rigid shell), and the interior. Two energy sources drive the system: internal heat (from planetary formation plus radioactive decay) powers plate tectonics and lithospheric motion; solar heat drives atmosphere/hydrosphere circulation.

Carbon cycle M p.550–551: carbon transfers among reservoirs — atmosphere, ocean, biosphere, and rock (as carbonate minerals and organic carbon) — over timescales from short-term biological exchange to long-term burial and weathering.

Energy sources M Ch.12: solar energy (direct, plus indirect via wind/biomass/hydropower) versus geothermal energy (Earth's internal heat, exploited where hot groundwater is accessible near the surface).

A-1.1 (Earth history) and hydrologic-cycle detail beyond the topographic-mapping content in §3.1 were confirmed present in Marshak (Ch.10–11, Interlude F) but not extracted in granular detail during source-checking — the material exists in your textbook if you want to build this section out further.

02 / Field Tools, Techniques & Measurement

A-3 through A-6

2.1

Subsurface Investigation — Drilling

Exploration drilling method selection depends on ground conditions and site sensitivity U Vol.1 Ch.8. Named methods: cable tool (percussion), direct rotary (with mud, water, or air-foam circulation), downhole hammer with reverse circulation, and augering (solid-stem or hollow-stem).

Worth knowing as a discrete fact: augering is the preferred method in the impervious core of an embankment dam specifically because it doesn't pressurize the borehole — the other methods all carry a real risk of hydraulic fracturing the embankment core if not carefully controlled. A hollow-stem auger also functions as its own casing, permitting sampling through the stem.

2.2

Rock and Soil Logging

Soil logging uses the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS) — per USBR 5000/5005, consistent with ASTM D2488 — as the standard descriptive framework for test pits, trenches, and drill holes U Vol.1 Ch.11. A separate system, USDA Soil Taxonomy, is used specifically for land-classification/irrigation-suitability work rather than general engineering logging.

Core logging centers on two standard quantitative metrics, computed for each core run:

  • Core recovery (CR) = (total length of core recovered ÷ total core run length) × 100
  • Rock Quality Designation (RQD) = (sum of length of intact core pieces ≥100 mm [4 in], measured along centerline ÷ total core run length) × 100 — a modified recovery percentage counting only sound, unbroken pieces, developed by D.U. Deere (1963). RQD >75% = good/high quality; <50% = poor quality (fractured/weathered). gen
Scope note, not a gap: USCS letter-codes (what "CH" or "SW-SM" actually decode to) and the plasticity-chart mechanics behind fine-grained soil classification are confirmed by ASBOG's own consolidated knowledge base to sit under Domain G (Engineering Geology), item G-2.2 — not this domain. RQD as a rock-mass classification tool is similarly G-3.2. This guide only covers the field-logging practice (A-3.2); the classification schemes themselves are being built out as a dedicated deck in the Domain G materials.

2.4

Borehole / Wireline Geophysics

  • Electric logs (SP, resistivity) — uncased, fluid-filled holes only; correlate strata, infer lithology/porosity/salinity U Vol.2 Ch.14
  • Nuclear logs (gamma-ray, gamma-gamma/density, neutron) — work in cased holes, unlike electric logs. High natural gamma count = shale/clay (K-40 source) U Vol.2 Ch.14
  • Acoustic borehole imaging (televiewer) — 360° acoustic image of the borehole wall; open discontinuities show as characteristic sinusoidal traces, from which strike and dip can be read directly U Vol.2 Ch.14
  • Caliper log — continuous borehole-diameter record; identifies washouts/fracture zones; corrects other logs for hole-diameter effects U Vol.2 Ch.14
2.5

Field Notes, Documentation & Record-Keeping

Base-map control, ranked most to least accurate U Vol.1 Ch.6: survey control/controlled photogrammetry → existing topographic maps → uncontrolled aerial photogrammetry → Brunton/tape survey → sketch mapping. Standard field equipment: Brunton compass, rock hammer, maps, aerial photos, field notebook, GPS, hand lens, tape measure.

2.6

GPS, Coordinate Systems & Datums

A datum is the reference surface for a coordinate system U Vol.1 Ch.6. USGS topographic maps historically used NAD 27; most modern surveys use NAD 83. Converting between them is not a simple coordinate transform — mixing datums without proper conversion produces real location errors, up to several hundred feet or meters.

GPS receiver accuracy varies enormously by equipment and method — from roughly 300 ft down to centimeters. Match the accuracy specified to the project's actual need, not to "what would be nice."

2.7

Scale and Scale Analysis

Map scale selection depends on project needs; USBR example ranges U Vol.1 Ch.6: regional reconnaissance mapping 1:24,000–1:62,500; detailed site mapping 1:250–1:12,000. Cross sections should generally use a natural scale (equal horizontal and vertical) — exaggerated vertical scale distorts geologic interpretation and is generally unsuited for structural analysis.

03 / Mapping, Remote Sensing & Interpretation

A-7 through A-9

3.1

Topographic & Geologic Maps — Strike, Dip & Apparent Dip

Topographic maps & profiles M Box F.1, p.389: contour lines connect points of equal elevation; the contour interval is constant on a given map — tightly spaced contours mean a steep slope, widely spaced means gentle. A topographic profile is the ground-surface trace along a vertical slice; add subsurface geology and it becomes a geologic cross section.

Strike and dip M Box 9.1, p.271: strike is the compass bearing of a horizontal line on a plane; dip is the angle of steepest descent, measured perpendicular to strike, from 0° (horizontal) to 90° (vertical).

Apparent dip: the dip of a surface as measured on a cross-section that is not perpendicular to strike. Apparent dip is always less than true dip; a section cut exactly perpendicular to strike shows true dip, and any oblique section shows a reduced, apparent value Waldron & Snyder, Lab 2.

3.2

Isopach & Isoconcentration Maps

Isopach map: a contour map connecting points of equal true stratigraphic thickness of a rock unit — thickness measured perpendicular to bedding, not straight down. A common point of confusion: an isochore map looks similar but measures true vertical thickness instead; the two are identical only when the unit is horizontal. Isopach maps are used to identify hydrocarbon reservoir volumes, basin geometry, and depocenters. gen

Isoconcentration map: a contour map connecting points of equal analyte concentration, built from lab data at monitoring-well locations — used to define the extent and shape of a groundwater contaminant plume. Contours are typically constructed so that regulatory-standard exceedance boundaries can be closed by interpolating between data points above and below the standard. gen

3.3

Aerial Photography & Traditional Remote Sensing

Aerial photography U Vol.1 Ch.6: panchromatic (full visible spectrum), natural color, false-color infrared (arbitrary colors, less haze-affected), and multispectral (multiple simultaneous bands). Photogrammetric mapping uses stereophotography to determine strike/dip and discontinuity geometry from photo pairs.

Other imaging systems U Vol.2 Ch.15:

  • Thermal infrared — images radiant surface temperature
  • Multispectral / hyperspectral scanners — capture many spectral bands simultaneously, used to identify mineral/rock spectral signatures
  • Satellite imagery (Landsat, SPOT) — regional-scale, synoptic coverage
  • Radar (SLAR) — active, weather/lighting-independent; radar interferometry can detect small topographic change remotely (e.g., landslide movement)
  • Sonar (side-scan, single/multi-beam) — underwater equivalent, used for reservoir/dam-bottom surveys

Two resolution types apply across all of these: spatial (sharpness / minimum detectable object size) and spectral (wavelength range and number of channels recorded).

3.4

LiDAR

Basic range equation F: R = c·(Δt/2), where c is the speed of light and Δt is the round-trip pulse time. Raw output is a 3D point cloud (elevation + return intensity); classifying that cloud yields a DSM (Digital Surface Model — everything the laser hit) or, after filtering, a bare-earth DEM (vegetation and structures stripped out) UOFR.

Three advantages over conventional photogrammetry F: (1) direct 3D measurement, versus photogrammetry's indirect derivation; (2) partial vegetation-canopy penetration, revealing bare-earth topography directly; (3) as an active sensor, largely independent of lighting and weather conditions.

Geologic payoff: bare-earth LiDAR reveals fault scarps, landslides, and alluvial fans that are invisible under vegetation in conventional aerial photography — directly relevant to Domain E paleoseismology as well as this domain's remote-sensing content.

3.5

Geographic Information Systems (GIS)

Vector vs. raster data models CS: vector represents discrete features — points, lines, polygons — with attribute data linked via an ID field to a separate table (e.g., a fault as a polyline, a sample site as a point). Raster represents space as a grid of cells, each holding one value; better suited to continuous data like elevation or spectral imagery.

Applied structure, from a real national geologic-mapping geodatabase UDS: feature classes by geometry type (polygons for geologic units, polylines for structures like faults/folds/dikes, points for sample sites) are linked to relational tables (Age, Lithology, References) via a shared key field. This is what enables an attribute-based spatial query — e.g., select all polygons where lithology = carbonate and rank = major — with the GIS automatically highlighting the matching map features.
