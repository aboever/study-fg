Geologic Timescale

Most recent at top. Every unit is labeled with its rank (EON / ERA / PERIOD / EPOCHS) so the level is never ambiguous, regardless of scroll position. Boundary ages sourced directly from the ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart, v.2026/06 (stratigraphy.org) — the current official standard. Precambrian boundaries are fixed by convention (GSSA), not fossil evidence, which is why they land on round numbers.

EonPhanerozoic 538.8 Ma–present
EraCenozoic 66.0 Ma–present
PeriodQuaternary2.58 Ma–present
EpochsHolocene (11.7 ka–present) · Pleistocene (2.58 Ma–11.7 ka)
Repeated glacial-interglacial cycles ("Ice Ages"); Homo sapiens emerges (~300 ka); Holocene begins with the end of the last glacial period — the entire span of recorded human civilization sits inside the Holocene.
PeriodNeogene23.04–2.58 Ma
EpochsPliocene (5.333–2.58 Ma) · Miocene (23.04–5.333 Ma)
Grasslands expand globally; hominid lineage diverges from other great apes (~7–6 Ma); Mediterranean nearly desiccates (Messinian Salinity Crisis).
PeriodPaleogene66.0–23.04 Ma
EpochsOligocene (33.9–23.04 Ma) · Eocene (56.0–33.9 Ma) · Paleocene (66.0–56.0 Ma)
Rapid mammalian radiation into niches vacated by dinosaurs; Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma) — abrupt global warming event; Antarctic ice sheet begins forming (Eocene-Oligocene boundary).
EraMesozoic 251.9–66.0 Ma
PeriodCretaceous143.1–66.0 Ma
Flowering plants (angiosperms) radiate; peak dinosaur diversity. Ends in the K-Pg extinction (Chicxulub impactor, Yucatán) — end of non-avian dinosaurs, ammonites, and most marine reptiles; the boundary itself is a globally traceable iridium-anomaly clay layer.
PeriodJurassic201.4–143.1 Ma
Dinosaurs dominate terrestrial ecosystems; Pangaea rifting produces the early Atlantic Ocean; first birds (Archaeopteryx).
PeriodTriassic251.9–201.4 Ma
Biotic recovery from the P-T extinction; first dinosaurs and mammals appear; Pangaea begins rifting. Ends in another Big Five extinction (Triassic-Jurassic), opening the way for dinosaur dominance.
EraPaleozoic 538.8–251.9 Ma
PeriodPermian298.9–251.9 Ma
Supercontinent Pangaea fully assembled. Ends in the Permian-Triassic extinction ("the Great Dying") — the most severe mass extinction in Earth history, ~90%+ of marine species lost, linked to Siberian Traps volcanism.
PeriodCarboniferous358.86–298.9 Ma
EpochsPennsylvanian (323.4–298.9 Ma) · Mississippian (358.86–323.4 Ma)
Extensive coal-forming swamp forests (the coal beds that give the period its name); atmospheric O₂ peaks unusually high (~35%), enabling giant arthropods; first reptiles (amniotic egg).
PeriodDevonian419.62–358.86 Ma
"Age of Fishes" — jawed fish diversify explosively; first forests and seed plants; first tetrapods (fish-to-amphibian transition) near the end. Ends in another Big Five extinction (Late Devonian).
PeriodSilurian443.1–419.62 Ma
First jawed fish; first vascular land plants; coral reefs diversify.
PeriodOrdovician486.85–443.1 Ma
Diversification of marine invertebrates continues (GOBE — Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event); first land plants (bryophyte-like); ends in one of the "Big Five" mass extinctions, tied to glaciation and sea-level fall.
PeriodCambrian538.8–486.85 Ma
The Cambrian Explosion — rapid diversification of nearly all major animal phyla with hard parts (trilobites, brachiopods) in a geologically short interval; first appearance of most animal body plans in the fossil record.
EonProterozoic 2.5–0.5388 Ga
EraNeoproterozoic 1.0–0.5388 Ga
  • Ediacaran (635–538.8 Ma): First macroscopic, complex multicellular life — the Ediacaran biota (soft-bodied, enigmatic organisms, e.g., Dickinsonia) — appears in the fossil record just after the Snowball glaciations end.
  • Cryogenian (720–635 Ma): "Snowball Earth" — global or near-global glaciation events (Sturtian, Marinoan), among the most severe ice ages in Earth history.
  • Tonian (1.0 Ga–720 Ma): Rodinia fully assembled, then begins rifting apart.
EraMesoproterozoic 1.6–1.0 Ga
  • Stenian (1.2–1.0 Ga): Supercontinent Rodinia begins assembling via the Grenville orogeny.
  • Calymmian–Ectasian (1.6–1.2 Ga): Supercontinent Columbia breaks up; first eukaryotic red algae fossils.
EraPaleoproterozoic 2.5–1.6 Ga
  • Statherian (1.8–1.6 Ga): Supercontinent Columbia (Nuna) assembling.
  • Orosirian (2.05–1.8 Ga): Major mountain-building episodes; large impact structures (Vredefort, Sudbury) date to this interval.
  • Rhyacian (2.3–2.05 Ga): Banded iron formations peak and then decline as dissolved ocean Fe²⁺ is progressively oxidized out; Huronian glaciation, possibly the first "Snowball Earth."
  • Siderian (2.5–2.3 Ga): Great Oxidation Event (GOE) begins — cyanobacterial photosynthesis starts raising free atmospheric O₂ for the first time.
EonArchean 4.031–2.5 Ga
EraNeoarchean 2.8–2.5 Ga
Peak crustal growth rate in Earth history; cyanobacteria proliferating, setting up the Great Oxidation Event just after this eon ends.
EraMesoarchean 3.2–2.8 Ga
Continued craton growth; earliest greenstone belts well preserved.
EraPaleoarchean 3.6–3.2 Ga
Oldest undisputed microfossils and stromatolites (~3.5 Ga, Pilbara Craton, Australia). Earliest continental crust beginning to stabilize.
EraEoarchean 4.031–3.6 Ga
Oldest confirmed rocks on Earth (Acasta Gneiss, Canada, ~4.03 Ga; Isua supracrustal belt, Greenland). Earliest possible chemical/isotopic evidence for life, contested.
EonHadean 4.567–4.031 Ga
Informal eon, no ratified lower boundary within it. Earth accretes (~4.567 Ga); Moon-forming giant impact; magma ocean solidifies; oldest known material on Earth is detrital zircon (Jack Hills, Australia, ~4.4 Ga), not a whole rock — no rock record survives from this eon.
Source and scope: boundary ages are the ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart, v.2026/06 (stratigraphy.org), fetched directly rather than reconstructed from memory. This page stops at Period/Epoch resolution — the ICS chart subdivides further into individual Ages/Stages (dozens more boundaries), which is beyond FG-exam scope; that level of granularity is a PG/professional-practice concern, not a conceptual one. Event descriptions are standard, well-established historical geology, not individually re-cited per event.
