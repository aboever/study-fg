01 / Hydrologic Cycle & Hydrostratigraphy

F-1

1.1

The Hydrologic Cycle

Water circulates among reservoirs — ocean, atmosphere, surface water, glacial ice, groundwater, and living organisms — in the never-ending hydrologic cycle Marshak Interlude F.4, pp.390–393. About 30% of ocean volume evaporates annually; ~76% of atmospheric water precipitates directly back into the ocean, the remainder onto land, where it infiltrates, runs off, or returns to the atmosphere by evapotranspiration (the sum of evaporation from water/ground surfaces plus plant/animal release) Marshak p.390.

At the regional scale, groundwater flow forms part of a larger steady-state system linking recharge areas, discharge areas, and groundwater divides — the framework used to quantitatively interpret flow systems and hydrologic budgets Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.6.1–6.2. A parallel, more approachable treatment of the cycle's groundwater role is in Poeter et al. 2020, Groundwater in Our Water Cycle, pp.17–22.

1.2

Aquifers, Aquitards & Hydrostratigraphy

An aquifer is sediment or rock with high porosity and permeability; an aquitard retards water movement without fully stopping it. An unconfined aquifer has no overlying aquitard; a confined aquifer is isolated from the surface by one Marshak p.476.

Formal USBR field definitions, useful for precise KB wording: aquiclude — relatively impermeable, absorbs water slowly, does not transmit significant groundwater (distinct from an aquitard, which is leaky); artesian aquifer — contains water under artesian (hydrostatic) pressure USBR Field Manual Vol.1 Ch.9, pp.245–246.

1.3

Saturated & Unsaturated Systems

The unsaturated zone (Marshak's term; USBR/ASBOG also use vadose zone or zone of aeration for the same thing) lies between the ground surface and the water table, where pores hold both air and water. The saturated zone below has all pores water-filled. The water table is the boundary between them; the capillary fringe is a thin zone just above it where surface tension pulls water up from the water table Marshak pp.477–478.

A perched water table forms above the regional water table where a lens of impermeable material (e.g., a shale lens in a sand aquifer) locally blocks downward percolation Marshak p.477. Negative pore-water pressure in the unsaturated zone is measured with a tensiometer; characteristic curves relate unsaturated hydraulic conductivity to moisture content Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.2.6.

1.4

Watershed Mass Balance & Processes

Evapotranspiration, infiltration, precipitation, and recharge are all defined and quantified in the reservoir-based treatment of the hydrologic cycle Marshak Interlude F.4, pp.390–393; steady-state hydrologic budgets formalize this at the watershed/basin scale, including recharge and discharge as explicit budget components Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.6.2.

Gap: sublimation (ice/snow → vapor, bypassing the liquid phase) is not named as a discrete water-balance term in Marshak, Freeze & Cherry, or the Groundwater Project water-cycle volume. The concept itself is basic (K–12 level) — just be aware ASBOG may test it as a named term in a mass-balance list without a deep source to cross-check against here.
1.5

Stream Discharge & Hydrographs

Discharge (Q) = cross-sectional area × average velocity, measured at stream-gauging stations Marshak Ch.14.3, pp.420–422. A hydrograph is a graph of discharge (or stage) versus time at a point on a stream FHWA-NHI-02-001 (2002), Highway Hydrology. Key hydrograph features: the rising limb (increasing discharge following a rain event), peak discharge, the recession (falling) limb, and baseflow — the sustained, groundwater-derived flow between storm events, separated from stormflow at the recession limb's inflection point.

Baseflow can also be separated using environmental isotopes rather than the recession curve alone — comparing the isotopic signature of storm runoff against baseflow's groundwater signature — a technique developed in full in Diamond 2022, Stable Isotope Hydrology (see §5.5).

1.6

Groundwater/Surface-Water Interaction

Whether a stream is permanent or ephemeral depends on whether the water table lies above or below the channel bed Marshak Ch.14.2, pp.420–421. Diverting surface water from a recharge area can lower the regional water table even where no groundwater is pumped directly — the Everglades case is Marshak's own example Marshak pp.484–485.

The exchange zone where surface water and groundwater actively mix is the hyporheic zone, treated in depth (streams, lakes, wetlands) in Woessner 2020, Groundwater-Surface Water Exchange; the same steady-state flow-system framework covers groundwater-lake interaction and baseflow recession specifically Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.6.5–6.7.

02 / Hydrogeologic Properties & Principles

F-2

2.1

Hydraulic Head & Hydraulic Gradient

Hydraulic head is the potential energy driving groundwater flow at a point, measured as the elevation to which water rises in a piezometer. Hydraulic gradient is the change in head per unit distance along the flow path: (h₁ − h₂)/l — roughly equal to the water table's slope Marshak Box 16.1, p.479. Groundwater flows from higher to lower head, not necessarily "downhill" in a topographic sense. A fuller conceptual/visual treatment of head is in Cohen & Cherry 2020, Conceptual and Visual Understanding of Hydraulic Head and Groundwater Flow.

2.3

Groundwater Flow Concepts

Darcy's Law: Q = KA(h₁−h₂)/l — discharge is proportional to hydraulic conductivity, cross-sectional area, and hydraulic gradient Marshak Box 16.1, p.479.

Two distinct velocity concepts are a classic point of confusion: specific discharge (Darcy flux, Q/A) is a bulk flow rate through the entire cross-sectional area, including solids — it is not the actual speed of water molecules. Average linear (seepage) velocity = specific discharge ÷ effective porosity, and is always larger than specific discharge because flow is confined to the pore space alone Devlin 2020, Groundwater Velocity, pp.15–18; Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.2.12.

2.4

03 / Groundwater Flow Systems

F-3

3.1

Groundwater Flow Systems & Flow Nets

Regional flow follows curved paths from recharge areas (under topographic highs) to discharge areas (under lows); some water travels deep before returning to the surface Marshak Fig.16.5, p.478. A flow net is a graphical grid of equipotential lines (constant head) and flow lines (perpendicular to them in isotropic media), used to visualize and quantify flow direction and rate Poeter & Hsieh 2020, Graphical Construction of Groundwater Flow Nets; Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.5; conceptual grounding in Cohen & Cherry 2020, pp.9, 11, 29–37.

3.2

Groundwater Flow Models

Numerical models predict aquifer response using finite-difference or finite-element methods to discretize and solve the groundwater flow equation over a grid; model calibration addresses the "inverse problem" of matching parameters to observed heads. Older analog simulation (electrical resistance-capacitance networks) exploited the mathematical equivalence between electrical and groundwater flow before digital methods became standard Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.8–8.9.

Beyond FG scope, but useful context: the dominant real-world tool implementing this theory is MODFLOW, the USGS's modular three-dimensional finite-difference groundwater flow model, first released in 1984 and now the de facto worldwide standard. Its defining innovation was a modular "package" architecture — separate, independently developed packages handle different boundary conditions and processes (e.g., the River Package, Recharge Package, Well Package, General-Head Boundary Package), sharing a common flow-solution core rather than requiring a monolithic custom code for each new problem. This modularity is why it displaced the earlier generation of purpose-built 2D and 3D codes (Pinder 1970; Prickett & Lonnquist 1971; Trescott 1975) that preceded it. Later versions extended the core code: MODFLOW-96, MODFLOW-2000 (added parameter-estimation processes), MODFLOW-2005, MODFLOW-NWT (Newton-Raphson solver, improved unconfined-aquifer stability), MODFLOW-USG (unstructured grids), and the current MODFLOW 6 (object-oriented rewrite, integrates prior variants into one framework). McDonald, Harbaugh & the original authors of MODFLOW 2003, "The History of MODFLOW," Groundwater 41(2), pp.280–283 — a peer-reviewed historical note written by MODFLOW's original developers; original program documentation in McDonald & Harbaugh 1988, USGS Techniques of Water-Resources Investigations, Book 6, Ch.A1.
3.3

Zones of Influence & Well Interference

Pumping draws the water table down around a well into a cone of depression; a deep well's drawdown can dry out shallower wells nearby Marshak p.480. The local gradient created by a cone of depression can be steep enough to reverse nearby groundwater flow direction — a mechanism by which septic-tank or other nearby contamination can be drawn into a pumping well Marshak pp.484–485. Formally, well interference occurs where multiple wells' cones of depression overlap; bounded aquifers (a nearby recharge or no-flow boundary, such as a stream or impermeable fault) distort the ideal radial drawdown pattern and are analyzed using image-well theory Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.3.

3.4

Karst & Fractured-Rock Flow Systems

Karst: limestone dissolution below the water table forms caves; if the water table drops, caves drain and speleothems (stalactites/stalagmites) precipitate from dripping water. Collapsed cave terrain produces sinkholes, natural bridges, and disappearing streams Marshak Ch.16.7, pp.487–491.

Fractured rock: joints and faults provide secondary porosity not present when the rock originally formed Marshak Fig.16.2c, p.475; fractured-rock flow behaves as a distinct medium from primary intergranular porosity, with flow concentrated along discrete discontinuities rather than distributed through the matrix Woessner & Poeter 2020, pp.7, 62, 71–74, 146–147; Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.2.12, Ch.9.2 ("Transport in Fractured Media").

04 / Hydrogeologic Methods of Investigation

F-4 (F-4.1 well drilling/construction is PG-only, excluded)

4.2

Laboratory & Field Hydraulic Tests

Field permeability tests fall into four types USBR Field Manual Vol.2 Ch.17, pp.107–165:

  • Pressure (packer) tests — one or two inflatable packers isolate a borehole interval; water is pumped in under pressure; used in stable rock
  • Constant-head gravity tests — no packers; water level held constant by continuous inflow
  • Falling-head gravity tests — packers used, only gravity head (no applied pressure)
  • Slug tests — a small, rapid water-level change; see §4.3

Laboratory permeameter tests on collected samples are generally less reliable than field tests because specimens are rarely truly undisturbed USBR Field Manual Vol.2 Ch.17, p.115; Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.4.

4.3

Single-Well Tests

Slug tests induce a rapid, small water-level change (by adding/removing a known volume of water, air, or a solid "slug") and track recovery to the static level; useful where the aquifer can't sustain a full pumping test or where introducing water risks water-quality impact. Two standard analyses: the Hvorslev method (assumes a homogeneous, isotropic, infinite medium; neglects wellbore storage) and the Bouwer-Rice method (accounts for finite wellbore storage; usable in confined, unconfined, or partially penetrating wells) USBR Field Manual Vol.2 Ch.17, pp.166–174; Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.5.

Specific capacity = well yield ÷ drawdown at that yield — a quick, approximate index of well/aquifer performance, distinct from a full aquifer test.

4.4

Multiple-Well (Pumping) Tests

A control well is pumped at a constant rate while drawdown is measured over time in one or more nearby observation wells. The classic solution for a fully confined, homogeneous, infinite aquifer is the Theis solution Theis 1935, Trans. AGU 16(2), matched to field data via log-log type-curve matching. The simplified Cooper-Jacob (semilog) method approximates the Theis solution for later time/small radius, plotting drawdown against log-time Cooper & Jacob 1946, Trans. AGU 27(4). Distinct solutions exist for leaky (aquitard-connected) and unconfined aquifers (the latter complicated by delayed gravity drainage) Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.3, 8.6. A recovery test — measuring residual drawdown after pumping stops — is considered an integral companion to any pumping test, not a separate procedure.

4.5

Pumping Test Boundary Conditions

Real aquifers are rarely infinite. A nearby recharge boundary (e.g., a hydraulically connected stream) slows drawdown below the ideal Theis curve; a nearby no-flow (impermeable) boundary (e.g., a fault or valley wall) accelerates it. Both are modeled using image-well theory — placing a hypothetical mirror-image well across the boundary (an injection well for a recharge boundary, a matching pumping well for a no-flow boundary) to reproduce the boundary's effect mathematically Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.8.3 ("Bounded Aquifers").

05 / Aqueous Geochemistry & Contaminant Hydrogeology

F-5 (F-5.4 remediation is PG-only, excluded)

5.1

Groundwater Geochemical Parameters

Natural groundwater quality issues Marshak covers directly: hard water (dissolved Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ from limestone/dolomite, causes scale and poor lathering), dissolved iron (rusty staining), hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg odor), and arsenic (from dissolving As-bearing minerals) Marshak p.486.

For the underlying chemistry: pH as a master variable in equilibrium calculations, and buffer intensity, are treated rigorously in Stumm & Morgan 1996, Ch.3, pp.88–144; alkalinity and the carbonate system in Stumm & Morgan 1996, Ch.4, pp.148–206; redox potential (Eh) and pE-pH (Pourbaix-type) diagrams, plus field measurement of redox potential, in Stumm & Morgan 1996, Ch.8, esp. §8.4 p.455, §8.5 p.464, §8.7 p.491. General field-index parameter measurement is also covered in Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.3.

5.2

Groundwater Contaminants

Sources Marshak names directly: agricultural waste (pesticides, fertilizers, animal sewage), industrial waste, landfill/septic effluent (including bacteria and viruses), petroleum products, radioactive waste, and acid mine drainage from sulfide minerals Marshak pp.484–486; a fuller sources-of-contamination taxonomy (land disposal, sewage disposal, agriculture, petroleum spills, deep-well injection) is in Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.9.5.

For organic contaminants specifically — the properties that govern their behavior in groundwater (chemical classes, solubility, Henry's Law/air-water partitioning, octanol-water partitioning) — see Mackay, Allen-King & Rixey 2024, Properties of Organic Contaminants, Ch.1–11.

5.3

AdvectionContaminant carried along with the bulk flow of groundwater at the average linear velocity

Transport-process theory: Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.9.2, 9.4. Diffusion/advection with Fick's Law derivations: McWhorter 2021, Flux Equations for Gas Diffusion in Porous Media, pp.2–55. Sorption mechanics and the sorption coefficient (K_d): Stumm & Morgan 1996, Ch.9 §9.7–9.9, pp.586–608 (§9.9 is titled specifically "Transport of (Ad)sorbable Constituents in Groundwater and Soil Systems"); Mackay, Allen-King & Rixey 2024, Ch.12, pp.82–94.

A breakthrough curve plots contaminant concentration at a fixed point versus time (or pore volumes) as a plume passes — the standard way transport/sorption behavior is visualized and interpreted from column or field tests Woessner 2020, pp.95–98, 116.

5.5

Isotopic & Tracer Studies

Radioactive (age-dating) tracers — parent isotope decays at a known rate; where both parent and a stable daughter can be measured (e.g., ³H decaying to stable ³He), summing them recovers the initial parent concentration for a groundwater age estimate. Tritium (³H) indicates recent recharge (decades-scale, half-life 12.3 yr); carbon-14 indicates older groundwater (thousands-of-years scale, half-life 5,730 yr) Cook 2020, Introduction to Isotopes and Environmental Tracers as Indicators of Groundwater Flow, §2.3 "Radioactive Tracers," pp.14, 42, 49, 51.

Stable isotopes — oxygen-18 and deuterium (²H) trace recharge source, elevation, and climate signal rather than providing decay-based dating; noble gases (e.g., dissolved Ne/Ar) provide an independent recharge-temperature proxy Cook 2020, §2.6–2.7, pp.74–82. Environmental tracers are also used for groundwater mixing analysis (end-member concentrations define a straight line for two-source mixing, a triangle for three-source) and for calibrating numerical groundwater-age models against measured tracer ages Cook 2020, Ch.3 ("Investigating Groundwater Mixing," "Calibrating Groundwater Models").

A dedicated, more mechanistic treatment of stable-isotope behavior — fractionation and distillation theory, meteoric water lines, the deuterium-excess parameter, and isotope-based hydrograph separation and recharge estimation — is in Diamond 2022, Stable Isotope Hydrology. General environmental-isotope grounding also in Freeze & Cherry 1979, Ch.3.8.
