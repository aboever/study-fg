01 / Stratigraphic Principles, Structures & Diagenesis

C-1, C-2 & C-3

1.1

Naming Conventions & Stratigraphic Units

Hierarchy of units Marshak p.173, 175, 312–313: a bed is a single layer with a recognizable top and bottom; the boundary between beds is a bedding plane; several beds together are strata; the overall layered arrangement is bedding/stratification. A distinctive sequence of beds traceable across a broad region is a stratigraphic formation — named after the locality where first identified (e.g., Kaibab Limestone, a single-rock-type formation; Toroweap Formation, multiple rock types, hence the word "formation" in the name). Several related formations comprise a group (e.g., Supai Group). The boundary surface between two formations is a contact.

Rock-unit naming for engineering purposes may be stratigraphic, lithologic, or genetic (e.g., Navajo Sandstone, Tertiary shale, metasediments); usage is normally informal (lower case) per the North American Stratigraphic Code, reserving formal capitalized names for previously-established formations USBR Vol.1 Ch.2, p.4.

1.2

Fundamental Stratigraphic Rules

PrincipleStatement
UniformitarianismPhysical processes observed today operated in the past at roughly comparable rates — "the present is the key to the past" Marshak p.306
Original horizontalitySediment layers are deposited approximately horizontal; tilted/folded beds were deformed after deposition Marshak p.306
SuperpositionIn an undisturbed sequence, each layer is younger than the one beneath it Marshak p.307
Lateral continuitySediment layers accumulate as continuous sheets; a canyon cutting a layer implies the layer once spanned the eroded area Marshak p.307
Cross-cutting relationsA feature that cuts across another is younger than the feature it cuts (dikes, faults) Marshak p.307
Baked contactsAn igneous intrusion metamorphoses ("bakes") the surrounding rock, so the baked rock is older than the intrusion Marshak p.307
InclusionsA rock containing a fragment of another rock is younger than that fragment Marshak p.307
Fossil successionA given fossil species occurs only within a limited stratigraphic interval and, once extinct, never reappears higher in the sequence Marshak p.308 — William Smith's discovery while surveying English canal excavations in the 1790s.
1.3

Unconformities

An unconformity is a surface representing a period of nondeposition and possibly erosion; the time gap it represents is a hiatus Marshak p.309.

TypeDefinition
Angular unconformityUnderlying rocks were tilted or folded before the unconformity formed — the orientation of layers differs above vs. below (e.g., Siccar Point, Scotland) Marshak pp.309–310
NonconformitySedimentary rocks overlie older intrusive igneous and/or metamorphic basement Marshak p.310
DisconformityParallel sedimentary sequences separated by an erosion surface (e.g., a sea-level fall exposes and erodes beds, then sea level rises and new sediment buries the surface) Marshak pp.310–311
ParaconformityA subtype of disconformity where the beds above and below are parallel with no visible erosional relief — recognized only via missing fossil zones or an age gap, not by field appearance. Short paraconformities are sometimes called diastems.
1.4

Stratigraphic Columns, Cross Sections & Fence Diagrams

A stratigraphic column summarizes the sequence of strata at a location, drawn to scale, divided into formations Marshak pp.312–313. A geologic map portrays the spatial distribution of formations and structures at the surface; a block diagram adds a 3D side-panel view showing subsurface geometry (e.g., an angular unconformity over folded strata beneath a map view) Marshak pp.313–315.

A fence diagram extends this to multiple locations: several stratigraphic columns or cross sections are drawn in their true relative geographic positions and their correlated units joined between them — a 3D "fence" of connected panels used to show how strata thicken, thin, or pinch out across a region.

Fence diagrams specifically are not illustrated in Marshak (which shows single columns and 2D geologic maps/block diagrams only) — the definition above is generic/uncontested rather than pulled from your textbook.
1.5

Geochronology & the Geologic Time Scale

The geologic column is a composite stratigraphic column built by correlating rocks worldwide, representing the entirety of Earth history Marshak p.315. Largest subdivisions: Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, Phanerozoic Eons (the first three together = Precambrian). The Phanerozoic subdivides into Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic Eras, then periods, then epochs. Period names derive from type localities (Devonian ↔ Devon, England) or characteristic rock (Carboniferous ↔ coal-bearing) — terminology grew haphazardly between 1760–1845, before numerical ages existed, so divisions are of unequal duration Marshak pp.315–316.

Numerical age is determined via isotopic (radiometric) dating: radioactive decay proceeds at a constant rate (the half-life), so the parent:daughter isotope ratio in a mineral gives its age Marshak pp.320–323.

1.6

Correlation

Lithologic correlation matches rock type between nearby stratigraphic columns (sometimes via a distinctive marker/key bed); fossil correlation uses fossils of the same relative age to correlate across broader areas where lithology changes with depositional environment Marshak pp.312–314. Species that are widespread but survived only briefly are index fossils (or guide fossils) — ideal correlation tools Marshak p.309.

Geomagnetic polarity correlation: Earth's magnetic field periodically reverses; a magnetic reversal flips normal↔reversed polarity, and the interval between reversals is a chron. The magnetic-reversal chronology (named chrons: Brunhes, Matuyama, Gauss, Gilbert) is built by isotopically dating rock layers at their measured paleomagnetic polarity, then used to correlate strata worldwide Marshak Ch.2, pp.46–47.

1.7

Sedimentary Structures — Primary, Secondary & Biogenic

Primary structures (formed during deposition) Marshak pp.173–177:

  • Cross-bedding — internal laminations within a bed, inclined at the angle of a bedform's slip face (ripples, dunes); orientation records current direction
  • Ripple marks — small, current-perpendicular ridges on a bed surface; dunes are the larger-scale equivalent
  • Graded bedding — grain size fines upward within a bed, produced by a turbidity current (deposit = turbidite)
  • Mud cracks — hexagonal, upward-curling plates formed when a mud layer dries and shrinks
  • Scour marks — small troughs eroded parallel to current flow

Flute marks and tool marks are sole marks — structures preserved on the underside ("sole") of a bed, at the contact with underlying mud. Flute casts are asymmetric, spoon-shaped scours cut by turbulent (often turbidity-current) flow, with a bulbous up-current end tapering downstream — a reliable paleocurrent and way-up indicator. Tool marks/groove casts are linear grooves gouged by an object (pebble, shell, wood) dragged along by the current; the "tool" itself is rarely preserved, only its groove.

Secondary structures (formed after deposition, during diagenesis):

  • Concretions — hard, compact, often spherical/ovoid masses formed by mineral cement precipitating around a nucleus within already-deposited sediment, usually early in burial history — distinct from a nodule, which forms by replacement rather than precipitation. Noted by USBR as a useful correlation/interpretation marker feature USBR Vol.1 Ch.4, p.70
  • Stylolites — serrated, suture-like surfaces formed by pressure dissolution: mineral matter is dissolved under differential stress, reducing rock volume; insoluble residue (clay, oxides) marks the surface. Common in limestones.
  • Liesegang banding — rhythmic, often iron-oxide (hematite/goethite) precipitation bands cutting across bedding, formed by a diffusion-reaction (supersaturation-nucleation-depletion) process rather than primary layering; band spacing increases geometrically outward from the diffusion front.

Biogenic structures: trace fossils — footprints, feeding traces, burrows, and dung (coprolites) — record organism activity rather than body parts Marshak Interlude E, p.295. Stromatolites are distinctive layered mounds built by cyanobacteria trapping sediment in a mucus-like mat, with new colonization building the mound upward; Archean examples date to ~3.2 Ga Marshak p.331.

Concretion, stylolite, and Liesegang-banding definitions cross-checked against the AGI Glossary of Geology, 4th ed. (Jackson, ed., 1997) — the standard reference USBR itself designates as its accepted terminology source outside the topics USBR defines independently USBR Vol.1 Ch.2, p.4.
1.8

Diagenesis

Diagenesis is the umbrella term for all physical, chemical, and biological processes that transform sediment into sedimentary rock and alter it afterward Marshak p.185:

  • Compaction — burial pressure squeezes out trapped water/air, packing clasts together
  • Cementation — minerals (commonly quartz or calcite) precipitate from groundwater and fill pore space between clasts
  • Lithification — the overall transformation of loose sediment to solid rock (compaction + cementation)
  • Recrystallization — new crystals grow at the expense of old ones
  • Replacement — one mineral is dissolved and a different mineral precipitates in its place (e.g., dolomite replacing calcite to form dolostone; cryptocrystalline quartz replacing calcite to form "replacement chert")
  • Dissolution — groundwater dissolves existing grains/cement

Diagenesis grades into true metamorphism between roughly 150°C and 300°C Marshak p.185.

Reduction-oxidation reactions in diagenesis: as organisms die and are buried, bacteria remineralize organic matter using a sequence of electron acceptors, consumed in order of decreasing free-energy yield per mole of organic carbon oxidized — aerobic respiration → denitrification → manganese reduction → iron reduction → sulfate reduction → methanogenesis. Each stage produces a characteristic pore-water profile (O₂ and NO₃⁻ depleted near the surface; dissolved Mn²⁺, then Fe²⁺, then sulfide accumulating with depth) Burdige 2006, pp.99–102, following the classic model of Froelich et al. (1979) and grounded in Berner's diagenetic redox-potential framework cited in Burdige 2006, p.102. The same electron-acceptor cascade is independently presented in Schulz & Zabel 2006, Ch.3.

02 / Facies Analysis & Depositional Environments

C-4 & C-5

2.1

The Facies Concept, Fabric & Walther's Law

A (sedimentary) facies is a group of rocks and primary structures indicative of a given depositional environment Marshak glossary. Walther's Law of Facies (Johannes Walther, 1894, translated by G. Middleton 1973) states that facies observed in vertical succession must represent depositional environments that were once laterally adjacent — a conformable vertical sequence records the lateral migration of environments through time (e.g., transgression stacking a beach facies beneath an offshore-mud facies as the shoreline moves inland).

Fabric: porosity is the fraction of a material's volume that is empty (pore) space; permeability is the degree to which a material allows fluid to pass through its interconnected pores Marshak Ch.16, pp.474–479; Ch.12, pp.356–357. Packing describes how closely grains are arranged — burial and diagenesis increase packing (e.g., "pore collapse," the closer packing of grains that occurs when groundwater is withdrawn, eliminating the support that had held them apart). Bedding and foliation are the features that give a rock body anisotropic properties — directionally dependent behavior, as opposed to isotropic (uniform in all directions) — because they represent potential failure surfaces and control property variation with orientation USBR Vol.1 Ch.4, pp.73–74.

2.2

Facies Changes & Relative Sea-Level Change

Transgression: relative sea level rises, the shoreline migrates inland, and coastal/marine facies overlap terrestrial ones. Regression: relative sea level falls, the shoreline migrates seaward Marshak pp.184–185. A vertical succession recording transgression shows a deepening-upward (fining-upward) trend (e.g., redbeds → sandstone → shale → coal, as the shore migrates inland over a floodplain); regression produces a mirror-image shallowing-upward (coarsening-upward) succession. This is a direct application of Walther's Law (§2.1) to a real dataset — Marshak's own worked example.

2.3

Clastic Environments — Textural Indicators, Non-Marine & Transitional

Textural indicators Marshak pp.166–167: clast size (boulder → cobble → pebble → sand → silt → clay); angularity (angular → subangular → subrounded → rounded — clasts round with transport distance); sphericity (how closely a clast approaches a sphere); sorting (uniform grain size = well sorted; mixed sizes = poorly sorted — sorting improves with transport distance and reworking).

Non-marine environmentDiagnostic character
GlacialUnsorted, unstratified "till" — clay to boulder size all mixed together (ice can transport any grain size)
Mountain streamBreccia and conglomerate; coarse clasts settle between floods
Alluvial fanWedge-shaped apron of gravel/sand at a mountain front; feldspar survives (arkose) since transport is short
Sand dune (eolian)Thick, well-sorted sandstone with large cross beds
Fluvial (river)Cross-bedded, rippled channel sand grading to floodplain silt/shale with mud cracks; lens-shaped channel geometry
Lacustrine (lake)Finely laminated shale from suspended clay settling in quiet water; small Gilbert-type deltas at stream mouths (topset/foreset/bottomset beds)
Marshak pp.178–179

Transitional environments: deltas — complex assemblages of swamp, channel, floodplain, and submarine-slope facies where a river enters standing water Marshak pp.179–181. Beaches — well-sorted, well-rounded, rippled sand, winnowed by wave action Marshak p.181. Barrier islands and tidal flats/lagoons — an elongate, wave-dominated sand ridge (barrier/strand plain) fronting a protected, low-energy lagoonal-tidal flat zone landward of it; these appear explicitly as facies belts on the shallow, wave-dominated part of a carbonate ramp profile McCave 2002, in Wefer et al. (eds.), Ocean Margin Systems, pp.1–14 — the same geomorphic zonation applies to siliciclastic coastlines generally.

2.4

Marine Clastic Environments — Shelf, Slope, Rise & Abyssal Plain

Shallow-marine: fine-grained, well-sorted, well-rounded silt with abundant marine fossils, deposited below wave-stirring depth. Deep-marine: turbidity-current graded beds near the shelf-slope transition; finely laminated mudstone (clay) and chalk/chert (plankton shells) far offshore Marshak pp.182–183.

Formal marine zonation, with numeric criteria Burdige 2006, pp.24–26: the continental margin (transition from oceanic to continental crust) divides into the continental shelf (seaward extension of the continent out to the shelf break, averaging ~130 m water depth but reaching 500 m), the continental slope below it, and the continental rise at its base — the slope/rise boundary occurs around 1,500–3,500 m (≈2,000 m average). Below the rise lies the ocean basin floor / abyssal plain, the transition typically at 3,000–5,000 m. Passive margins (e.g., Atlantic) are generally wider than active margins (e.g., Pacific).

Pelagic sediments are essentially synonymous with deep-sea sediments, settling out of the water column; hemipelagic sediments occur along the lower continental margin in shallower water, with a larger contribution from lateral transport processes like turbidity currents Burdige 2006, p.25. Coastal/nearshore sediments are shelf sediments close enough to land to be strongly land-influenced — estuaries, bays, lagoons, and river deltas Burdige 2006, p.25.

2.5

Carbonate Environments — Platform, Ramp & Pelagic

Shallow-water carbonate production: beaches collect shell-fragment sand; lagoons (protected, quiet water) accumulate carbonate mud; reefs consist of coral and coral debris, with a sloping apron of reef fragments offshore Marshak p.182.

The formal classification distinguishes two fundamentally different carbonate platform geometries Read 1985, AAPG Bulletin 69(1), 1–21:

TypeProfile
Rimmed shelf/platformRelatively flat top with a marked break in slope at a high-energy shelf edge (commonly reef-rimmed), passing abruptly into deep water — a protected shelf lagoon lies behind the rim
Ramp — homoclinalLow-angle profile passing seaward into deeper water with no major break in slope; no deep-water breccias
Ramp — distally steepenedLow-angle proximally, but with an offshore slope break between the shallow ramp and the basin; slope facies may contain breccias of slope-derived clasts

Ramps subdivide further into inner-, mid-, and outer-ramp zones, bounded by fair-weather and storm wave base. A corroborating depositional-profile diagram — independently sourced, from a different reference you own — shows the ramp's protected back-ramp zone hosting lagoonal-tidal-flat facies (with associated evaporites/paleosols/paleokarst) and the shallow wave-dominated zone hosting beach/barrier/strand-plain facies, exactly matching the rimmed-shelf-vs-ramp framework above McCave 2002, in Wefer et al. (eds.), Ocean Margin Systems, pp.1–14.

Pelagic carbonate: chalk, formed from plankton shells settling like snow through open water, far from clastic or shallow-shelf influence Marshak p.183.

2.6

Evaporite Environments

Salt precipitates where saltwater becomes supersaturated — evaporation removes water faster than new water enters — in desert lakes and along the margins of restricted seas Marshak pp.169–171. The specific mineral depends on the degree of evaporation: gypsum forms once ~80% of the water has evaporated; halite once ~90% has evaporated. Classic example: the Bonneville Salt Flats, Utah, formed by evaporation of an ancient salt lake.

03 / Fossil Record & Basin Analysis

C-6 & C-7

3.1

Fossil Record, Evolution & Connections to the Time Scale

Fossilization requires special circumstances: an anoxic (oxygen-poor) environment slows decay, rapid burial pre-empts rotting/scavenging, and the organism needs durable hard parts Marshak pp.293–294. Preservation modes include preserved/replaced bones and shells, molds and casts, carbonized impressions, permineralization (e.g., petrified wood), trace fossils, and chemical fossils/biomarkers Marshak pp.295.

Taxonomy: three domains (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya); Eukarya divides into kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia), then phylum → class → order → family → genus → species Marshak pp.297–298. Evolution proceeds by natural selection (Darwin); debate continues over gradualism (slow, constant rate) vs. punctuated equilibrium (long stasis, brief rapid change) Marshak p.301. Mass extinction events mark several major geologic-period boundaries (e.g., end-Cretaceous, end-Permian) Marshak p.302.

The geologic column's structure is directly mapped onto life's evolutionary record — the Precambrian-Cambrian boundary is defined by the sudden appearance of shelled invertebrates (the Cambrian explosion, ~530 Ma); first fish appear in the Ordovician, land plants spread in the Silurian, amphibians in the Devonian, dinosaurs in the Triassic, and the end-Cretaceous extinction (65 Ma) closes the Mesozoic ("Age of Dinosaurs") and opens the Cenozoic ("Age of Mammals") Marshak pp.316–317.

3.2

Index Fossils & Biostratigraphy

An index fossil (guide fossil) is a species that is both widespread and stratigraphically short-lived — ideal for associating strata with a specific time interval Marshak p.309. The underlying discipline is biostratigraphy: "the element of stratigraphy that deals with the distribution of fossils in the stratigraphic record and the organization of strata into units on the basis of their contained fossils" North American Stratigraphic Code, Glossary of Stratigraphic Terms — this is the formal name for the fossil-succession concept Marshak teaches thoroughly (§1.2, §1.6) without using the term itself.

3.3

Sequence Stratigraphy

Founded by Peter Vail, Robert Mitchum, and colleagues at Exxon, published in AAPG Memoir 26 (1977). A depositional sequence is bounded above and below by unconformities (subaerial unconformities and their correlative conformities). Within a sequence, systems tracts represent packages deposited during a specific stage of relative sea-level change:

Systems tractWhen deposited
Lowstand (LST)During/just after relative sea-level fall reaches its lowest point
Transgressive (TST)During relative sea-level rise, shoreline migrating landward
Highstand (HST)Near/after maximum sea level, progradation resumes
Falling-stage (FSST)During relative sea-level fall, before the next sequence boundary

These are bounded by named surfaces — the sequence boundary (subaerial unconformity + correlative conformity), the maximum flooding surface (separating TST from HST), and the maximum regressive/transgressive surface and basal surface of forced regression Van Wagoner et al. 1988, 1990 (AAPG). A parasequence is "a relatively conformable succession of beds or bedsets bound by marine-flooding surfaces" Van Wagoner et al. 1988 — the basic building block within a systems tract.

3.4

Seismic Stratigraphy

Seismic stratigraphy interprets depositional history from patterns in how seismic reflections terminate against surfaces — the basis for mapping sequences and systems tracts without well control Mitchum, Vail & Thompson 1977 (AAPG Memoir 26):

Termination typeGeometry / meaning
OnlapReflection terminates updip against a more steeply inclined underlying surface — deposition stopped against a topographic rise
DownlapInclined reflection terminates basinward against a flatter underlying surface — classic prograding-clinoform signature
ToplapLow-angle, non-erosional termination against an overlying surface — accommodation ran out, deposition simply stopped
TruncationErosional termination against an overlying surface — the most reliable indicator of a sequence boundary

Onlap and downlap terminate against the surface below them; toplap and truncation terminate against the surface above them. A sequence boundary is typically marked by truncation/toplap below and onlap above; a maximum flooding surface is marked by regional downlap.

3.5

Depositional Systems & Facies Architecture

A depositional system is a three-dimensional assemblage of genetically related facies acting together (e.g., a fluvial system = channel + point bar + floodplain + crevasse-splay facies) — the organizing concept between an individual facies and a full basin fill, originating with Fisher, Brown, Scott & McGowen at the Bureau of Economic Geology, University of Texas.

Facies architecture — Andrew Miall's "architectural-element analysis" — subdivides deposits into a hierarchy of internal bounding surfaces and recurring 3D facies assemblages ("architectural elements": channels, bars, bedforms, macroforms, overbank deposits), originally developed for fluvial systems and later extended to eolian and deep-water/turbidite systems Miall 1985, Earth-Science Reviews 22, 261–308.

3.6

Global Cycles — Milankovitch Cycles

Three orbital parameters combine to drive cyclical changes in the seasonal/latitudinal distribution of solar insolation, pacing glacial-interglacial cycles Marshak pp.538–540:

  • Eccentricity — Earth's orbital shape cycles between more circular and more elliptical, on a ~100,000-year period
  • Obliquity (tilt) — axial tilt varies, changing seasonal contrast
  • Precession — the axis wobbles like a spinning top, on a ~23,000-year cycle, changing which season occurs at perihelion
3.7

Provenance

Provenance analysis reconstructs the source-rock assemblage and tectonic setting that produced a sediment, from its detrital composition. The standard tool is the QFL diagram — a ternary plot of quartz (Q), feldspar (F), and lithic fragments (L) — on which sandstone compositions cluster by tectonic provenance type: continental block (craton interior/quartz-rich, transitional/arkosic), magmatic arc (volcanic-lithic-rich), and recycled orogen (chert/sedimentary-lithic-rich, from uplifted fold-thrust belts) Dickinson & Suczek 1979, AAPG Bulletin 63, 2164–2182. This directly extends the clast-composition-vs-weathering content Marshak already covers (breccia → conglomerate → arkose → quartz sandstone, as feldspar progressively weathers to clay with transport) Marshak pp.166–167 into a formal tectonic framework.
