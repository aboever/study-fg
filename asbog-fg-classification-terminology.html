Rock & Mineral Classification, and Confusing Terms

Sulfide/carbonate/oxide-type mineral classes are intuitive because the category name describes the chemistry. Silicate mineral names and rock-name pairs often don't work that way — this page exists to give the underlying taxonomy explicitly, and to call out specific name collisions before they cost you a question.

Rock classification, top level

Three classes, defined by how the rock formed — not by what it looks like.

Igneous — crystallized from molten rock (magma/lava)
Intrusive (plutonic) — cooled slowly underground → coarse-grained (granite, gabbro)
Extrusive (volcanic) — cooled quickly at the surface → fine-grained/glassy (rhyolite, basalt)
Classified further by silica content (felsic→mafic→ultramafic) and, when mineralogy is known, by the QAPF diagram; by bulk chemistry alone (e.g., altered/glassy rocks), by the TAS diagram instead — see the 3.4 sourcing note below.
Sedimentary — formed from compacted/cemented particles, or chemical/biological precipitation
Clastic — broken fragments of preexisting rock (sandstone, shale, conglomerate)
Chemical — precipitated directly from solution (rock salt, some limestone)
Biochemical — built from organic/skeletal material (most limestone, coal)
"Facies" here means a body of rock with characteristics reflecting a specific depositional environment (reef facies, deltaic facies) — see the facies disambiguation box below; this is NOT the same use of the word as metamorphic facies.
Metamorphic — preexisting rock (the protolith) altered by heat/pressure/fluids without melting
Foliated — minerals aligned/banded from directed pressure: slate → phyllite → schist → gneiss, in order of increasing grade
Nonfoliated — no directional pressure, or minerals that can't align: hornfels, quartzite, marble
Classified further by metamorphic facies — see the dedicated section below.

Mineral classification, top level

Every mineral belongs to one chemical class first. This is the part that's already intuitive for you (sulfides, carbonates, oxides) — silicates are the one class that then gets subdivided again by structure, which is where names stop being self-explanatory.

Non-silicate classes (name usually IS the chemistry)
Native elements (gold, copper, diamond/graphite)
Sulfides (pyrite, galena, sphalerite)
Oxides (hematite, magnetite, corundum)
Carbonates (calcite, dolomite)
Sulfates (gypsum, anhydrite, barite)
Halides (halite, fluorite)
Silicates — the class where names stop describing the chemistry directly, because they're grouped by how SiO₄ tetrahedra link together, not by which elements are present
Nesosilicates (isolated tetrahedra) — olivine, garnet, and the Al₂SiO₅ polymorph trio (kyanite, andalusite, sillimanite — see dedicated section below)
Inosilicates (chains) — single-chain = pyroxene group; double-chain = amphibole group (hornblende lives here — see disambiguation box)
Phyllosilicates (sheets) — micas (biotite, muscovite), clay minerals
Tectosilicates (3D framework) — quartz, feldspar group — see dedicated section below

Feldspar and plagioclase

This is the specific gap flagged from last session — feldspar only ever appeared in table rows, never explained as a concept.

The hierarchy

Feldspar is the group name (a tectosilicate framework mineral group, and the single most abundant mineral group in Earth's crust). It splits into two subgroups by composition:

SubgroupCompositionCommon members
K-feldspar (alkali feldspar)KAlSi₃O₈Orthoclase, microcline, sanidine
PlagioclaseNaAlSi₃O₈ (albite) – CaAl₂Si₂O₈ (anorthite), a continuous solid-solution seriesAlbite, oligoclase, andesine, labradorite, bytownite, anorthite

Plagioclase is not one fixed mineral — it's a compositional continuum between two end-members (albite and anorthite), and the specific name changes with the Na:Ca ratio (An%, the mole percent anorthite component):

NameAn% (anorthite content)
Albite0–10%
Oligoclase10–30%
Andesine30–50%
Labradorite50–70%
Bytownite70–90%
Anorthite90–100%
Marshak (2013), Ch. 3 mineral tables; Klein & Dutrow (2007) feldspar group chapter

The Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs — why they're not "a silly list"

Kyanite, andalusite, and sillimanite are polymorphs — identical chemical formula (Al₂SiO₅), different crystal structure, because each is the stable structural form under a different pressure-temperature regime. That's the whole reason they matter: find one of these three minerals in a metamorphic rock, and you've directly read off the P-T conditions that rock experienced, without needing any other evidence.

MineralIndicates
KyaniteHigh pressure
AndalusiteLow pressure, moderate temperature
SillimaniteHigh temperature

This is a direct application of metamorphic facies thinking (below) at the single-mineral scale — these three are sometimes called "index minerals" for exactly this reason.

Marshak (2013) p.200, Box 7.1

Metamorphic facies

A metamorphic facies is a group of metamorphic mineral assemblages that develop under a specified range of temperature and pressure conditions. It's a way of reading a rock's P-T history from its mineralogy, the same logic as the Al₂SiO₅ polymorphs above, extended to whole mineral assemblages rather than one mineral.

FaciesTypical setting
ZeoliteLowest grade, shallow burial
GreenschistRegional metamorphism, moderate grade
BlueschistHigh pressure, low temperature — specifically accretionary prisms at subduction zones
AmphiboliteRegional metamorphism, higher grade
GranuliteHighest grade regional metamorphism
EclogiteVery high pressure, subducted oceanic crust
HornfelsContact (thermal) metamorphism — NOT a pressure-driven facies, purely heat-driven; see disambiguation box, this is also a rock name
Marshak (2013) p.200, Box 7.1

Name collisions — terms that sound related and aren't

These are the specific pairs flagged as confusing. Mineralogy/petrology terminology was not built on a consistent naming logic for a beginner — these overlaps are real, not a sign anything's being missed conceptually.

Rock vs. Mineral — not related despite the shared root

Hornfels vs. Hornblende

Hornfels — a ROCK. Fine-grained, nonfoliated metamorphic rock formed by contact metamorphism around an igneous intrusion. Mineral content varies by protolith.Marshak (2013) p.195
Hornblende — a MINERAL. A common dark green-to-black amphibole group mineral (double-chain silicate), found in both igneous and metamorphic rocks. Not specific to hornfels at all.standard mineralogy, amphibole group
Same word, two unrelated technical meanings

Facies (sedimentary) vs. Facies (metamorphic)

Sedimentary facies — a body of rock with characteristics (texture, composition, fossils) reflecting the depositional environment it formed in (e.g., "reef facies," "deltaic facies").
Metamorphic facies — a mineral assemblage indicating the pressure-temperature conditions of metamorphism (greenschist, amphibolite, etc. — see above).Marshak (2013) p.200

Both are legitimate, standard uses of the word "facies" in geology — the term itself just means "aspect" or "appearance," and geologists apply it to two completely different phenomena. Context (sedimentary rocks vs. metamorphic rocks) tells you which meaning is in play.

Foliation intensity gradient, easy to blur together

Slate → Phyllite → Schist → Gneiss

Not four unrelated rocks — one continuum of increasing metamorphic grade, same general (shale/mudstone) protolith. Slate: fine, dull, splits into flat sheets. Phyllite: slightly coarser, silky sheen. Schist: coarse enough to see individual mica flakes, strongly foliated. Gneiss: highest grade, compositional banding rather than mica-flake foliation.

Marshak (2013) Ch.7
Both feldspars, different subgroup — see the dedicated section above for the full breakdown

Orthoclase vs. Plagioclase

Orthoclase is one specific K-feldspar mineral. Plagioclase is the entire Na-Ca solid-solution series, not a single mineral. They're not parallel terms at the same level — orthoclase is a species, plagioclase is a whole compositional range.

Both dark, chain-silicate mineral groups — distinguished by cleavage angle

Pyroxene vs. Amphibole

Pyroxene = single-chain silicate, cleavage at ~90°. Amphibole (hornblende's group) = double-chain silicate, cleavage at ~56°/124°. Both are common dark ("mafic") rock-forming minerals and easy to conflate at a glance — the cleavage angle is the reliable field discriminator.

Sourced from last session's flagged gap (Domain B, section 3.4)

Porphyry alteration zonation

Concentric hydrothermal alteration zones around a porphyry intrusion, moving outward from the causative stock: potassic (innermost — K-feldspar, biotite, magnetite) → phyllic (sericite, pyrite, quartz) → argillic/advanced argillic (clay minerals, often overprinting the other zones from above) → propylitic (outermost — chlorite, epidote, albite, calcite). Copper mineralization concentrates near the potassic-phyllic boundary.

Lowell & Guilbert 1970 (original model); Sillitoe 2010 (modern revision)

CIPW normative mineralogy

A calculation, not a mineral assemblage you observe directly — it converts a rock's bulk chemical analysis (oxide weight percentages) into a standardized set of hypothetical "normative minerals," assuming anhydrous crystallization at low pressure. This lets geologists compare igneous rocks on a consistent basis regardless of actual cooling history or crystallinity (e.g., comparing a glassy volcanic rock to a coarse plutonic rock of similar chemistry). Not to be confused with the mode, the rock's actual observed mineral content.

Cross, Iddings, Pirsson & Washington, early 1900s; Britannica, "CIPW norm"

QAPF vs. TAS diagrams

QAPF (Quartz – Alkali feldspar – Plagioclase – Feldspathoid) classifies igneous rocks by their actual mineral content (mode) — usable only when minerals can be identified, which generally means coarse-grained/plutonic rocks. TAS (Total Alkali-Silica) classifies by bulk chemistry instead, for rocks where minerals can't be resolved — fine-grained, glassy, or altered volcanic rocks. Same underlying goal (name the rock), different input data depending on what's actually observable in the specimen.

Streckeisen 1973/1976 (QAPF); Le Bas et al. 1986 (TAS)
